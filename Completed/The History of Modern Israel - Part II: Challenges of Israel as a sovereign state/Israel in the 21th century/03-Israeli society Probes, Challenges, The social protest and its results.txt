When considering the economic growth,
there are serious potentially volatile social implications
that must be taken into consideration. The poverty rate in Israel is above 20%, one of the highest rates
among OECD countries, which Israel became a member of in 2010. More than 30% of Israeli children
living under the poverty line. Within the Arab and
Ultra-Orthodox segment of the society, more than 50% of the families
lived in poverty. Half of the Israeli labor force
does not reach the minimum wage that would enable them decent living. The rising prices of real estate
make it almost impossible for young Israelis to buy a house. Many segments of the society such as the
ultra-orthodox part of the Arab community, newcomers, and low middle-class groups
are not involved in the growing economy and experience a deteriorating,
downward mobility. Many Israeli youngsters feel that
they have no future in the country if such economic development
would continue and are looking for
options to leave the state. Moreover, many Israeli feel
that the lack of vision and corruption took over the Israeli politics
in public sector in the last year. Such a feeling raises distrust and mutual
suspicion among the various segments of the society, leading to losing sense
of solidarity between the citizens. It seems to many people that
Israel has become a Plutocracy rather than a democracy. They are worried that the state is
controlled by a small group of tycoons who promote their private interest, instead
of democratically elected politician, who are supposed to act on
behalf of the public interest. This concern over
the growing misconduct and inequality generate a mass movement for
protest in the summer of 2011 that called for
social justice in the country. It began with the protest against
the rising cost of living and in particular against
the real estate market. It spread through social media and culminated in huge demonstrations
in Tel Aviv and other cities. Hundreds of thousands of
people demonstrated against the general hardships that so
many Israelis are facing and assign responsibility on governmental,
social, and economic policy. The protesters built tent cities
at central avenues and parks and stayed there for
many weeks before evacuating. The government appointed a committee that
eventually recommended some targeted changes in social policy aimed
to alleviate the hardships. Some of the recommendations were
implemented by the government, like free education from the age of three. Most of the recommendations, however,
were rejected, ignored or postponed. The protest spirit has a battered sense,
and its overall impact on public
atmosphere is still under debate. Some of the protest leaders
became members of the Knesset. Others refused to
collaborate with the system. The social discontent still
prevails among many Israelis. Though no similar grassroots
protest re-emerged. This fact can be partly explained
by deep disappointment and feeling of alienation and
even aversion to certain path of the Israeli Society from the political
system and the whole in recent year. Many Israelis believed that the current
political structure impedes any possibility for social reform and
thus apathy and cynicism won over reform spirit and
hope for change
