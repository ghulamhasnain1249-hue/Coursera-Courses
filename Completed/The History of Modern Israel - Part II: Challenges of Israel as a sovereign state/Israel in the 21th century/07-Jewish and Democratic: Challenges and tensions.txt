In sum, Israel is a country that faces and deals with many contradictions and
paradoxes. It seems that the core of many
of the issues facing Israeli society today lies the inherent
contradiction in Israel's self definition as a Jewish and democratic state. Many questions arise concerning
these official definitions. Some challenge it's Jewish nature, and others question it's
democratic characteristics. Regarding the Jewish
identity of the country, some may ask What defines a Jew? Religious practices, biological origins, national collective identity,
cultural heritage or personal feeling? What is the definition of a Jewish state? Does it simply imply a state for
the Jews or rather a state where Jewish
religious laws are part and parcel. Of it's legal system may
be even superior to the civic law. Does Jewish mean practicing religion? What does it mean about a citizen. A citizenship status of
a non Jewish minorities. Which are more than 20% of
the Israeli population. How can Israel claim to represent the Jews if it does not recognize the Jewishness
of reform and conservative Judaism which together comprise the largest
Jewish denominations in North America? Who is the official body that will
determine the Jewish nature of the state? Regarding the democratic identity, many would ask difficult questions
such as, how can a country that holds millions of Palestinian people
under occupation since 1967, denying them of political and
civil rights view itself as a democracy. Can the Law of Return that grants
automatic citizenship to any Jew who wished to settle in Israel be considered
a discriminatory law against non-Jews? How can a democracy, which requires
mandatory military service by law, exempt a significant portion
of its population and thereby denying them some social and
economic benefits and denies them the access to key
positions in Israeli society. How can a country that
prohibits the occupation of some official positions by non
Jews be viewed as a democracy? These serious and crucial questions
challenge the Israeli claim of being both Jewish and democratic. Yet only at the far extremes of the Israel
society we can find individuals and groups that advocate
breaking this association. Among extreme right religious groups,
there are rabbis and other followers who aspire to
change Israel into a theocracy. They hope to make Jewish religious law,
the law of the country and to build the third Jewish temple
as a step towards the reenactment of the biblical Kingdom of Israel. For them, democracy is an obstacle, a
foreign element that should be abandoned. On the other side most of the Arab
population of the country, as well as certain left wing
Jewish organizations seek to make Israel a state
of it's all citizens. This statement means that Israel should
adopt democratic standards of citizenship. Regardless of Jewish particularities
that are at odd with these standards. According to them, the country should keep
strict separation between religion and politics and make the public
feel culturally neutral and void of any Jewish characteristics. Each of these two groups has its logical
reasons for changing the nature of the state and solving the impossible and
unbridging conflicts between Jewish and Democratic. But most citizens of the country, as you
have probably seen throughout the course, are living quite peacefully with
this contradictory definition. They prefer such a hybrid
identity of Jewish and democratic over a pure Jewish theocracy,
lacking democratic practices and values. Or a pure, archetypal democracy void
of any Jewish public characteristics.
