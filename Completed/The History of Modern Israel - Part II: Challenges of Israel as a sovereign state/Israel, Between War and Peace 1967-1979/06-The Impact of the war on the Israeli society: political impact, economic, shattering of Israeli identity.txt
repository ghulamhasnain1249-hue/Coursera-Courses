Despite the military victory
at the end of the war, the Yom Kippur War shattered and
shocked Israeli society. An entire generation of young
warriors literally had to hold back the surprising Egyptian and
Syrian invasion, and paid a heavy toll. 2,365 dead, more than 6,000 wounded, and hundreds of soldiers captured. The general public grieved its losses and lost faith in the political and
military leadership. The war was a tragic reminder of
Israel's extreme vulnerability, which had been forgotten since
the victory of the Six-Day War. It became clear how dependent
Israel was on the United States for weapons and financial aid. It also became clear that the Arab
economic weapon, the oil, was an effective instrument for
political pressure on Israel. This diplomatic pressure
forced Israel to withdraw from all territories it
occupied at the end of the war. Many countries yield to
the oil boycott and cut their diplomatic relations with Israel. The crisis of faith caused by
the Yom Kippur War led to an outburst of protest initially headed by a few
reserve officers and soldiers. They called the defense minister to
take personal responsibility and resign. The protest led to a formation
of a national committee of inquiry that would investigate
what had gone wrong. The committee found the military
echelon responsible and called for the impeachment of the chief of staff, the head of the intelligence,
and other top generals. It spared the political leadership. But due to ongoing public pressure, Prime
Minister Golda Meir resigned from office and was replaced in 1974 by Yitzhak Rabin,
Israeli Ambassador to the United States and the chief
of staff during the Six-Day War. The Yom Kippur oversight put in
motion many economic, political and social trends which had an enormous
long term impact on Israel. Politically, the war precipitated the political turnover of 1977 that brought to power the right-wing Likud party under
the leadership of Menachem Begin, thereby ending more than 50
years of Labor Party hegemony. Economically, the war caused
extreme economic hardship. The need to reconstruct the army required
an increase in the military budget, at the expense of many other services. The rise in energy prices
severely affected the economy, that ended a significant
period of stagflation, inflation and stagnation. Militarily, the cease-fire was
signed between Israel and Egypt, and Israel and Syria, at the end of the war. But many violent incidents
took place between Palestinian fighter and the Israeli army. The PLO that was founded
in 1964 increased its terrorist activities against
Israel after the war, both within its border and abroad. They killed civilians in many villages and
towns, hijacked civic airplanes, and
attacked official Israeli targets abroad, such as the Israeli Olympic
team in Munich. The most well-known event recurred
in 1976 when Palestinians hijacked an Air France airplane and
forced it to land in Uganda, separating and holding Jewish and
Israeli passengers as hostages. In a brilliant operation, an elite
unit of the IDF released the hostages, killed the kidnappers, and brought back
most passengers from Uganda to Israel. In March 1978, a devastating attack was carried out on a bus
travelling on the Coastal Road. It triggered an intensive military
response by Israel against Palestinian bases in Lebanon, the Litani Operation. Overall, the Yom Kippur War
changed Israeli mood. The euphoria turned into melancholy and deep suspicion towards
the leaders of the country. The war demonstrated
the limits of Israel's power. It seems that despite its technological
and military supremacy over its enemy, the country underwent a crisis of
confidence that stirred emotions of malaise and dissatisfaction among
many segments of the society. Various civic,
non-governmental organizations emerge after the war in order to influence
the Israeli political and public agenda. The most famous of them were the
Settlement Movement known as Gush Emunim, and the opposite Peace Now movement advocating withdrawal from
the occupied territories.
