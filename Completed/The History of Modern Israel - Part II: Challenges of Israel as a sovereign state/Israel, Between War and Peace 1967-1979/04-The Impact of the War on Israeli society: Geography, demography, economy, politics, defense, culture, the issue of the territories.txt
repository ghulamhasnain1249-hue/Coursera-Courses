The Six-Day War had
an enormous impact on Israel. There are historians,
economics, sociologists, and other expert who view it as
the historical turning point that completely changed every
aspect in the life of the country. The Israeli public was euphoric
to the point of intoxication. And some Israelis perceived the victory
as a messianic, divine intervention. The country expanded its border. Israel captured new territories. In the north, the Syrian height which
was changed to the Golan height, in the East the Western bank which
will change to Judea and Samaria. And in the South the Gaza strip and
the Sinai peninsula. These borders provided
a new sense of security. Control of the Golan Heights secured
water sources to the country and the top of the Hermon Mountain
provided a new strategic asset. Control over the vast area of Sinai
created a buffer zone in the ground and in the air and added a wealth of touristic
sites and natural resources such as oil. Control of the West Bank provided broad
defensive barrier between Israel and the Arab states east of the Jordan
such as Jordan and Iraq. It gave Israelis access to Jewish
holy sites like the Cave of the Patriarchs near Hebron and
Rachel's Tomb near Bethlehem. The impact of the conquest of East
Jerusalem exceeded security advantages. Its major significance for many Jews was religious,
cultural, and emotional. It made it possible to unify
the capital of the country, and provided access to the Wailing Wall,
the holiest site of Judaism. Capturing East Jerusalem and the Western
Wall evoked an emotional response. It seems that the ancient
dream of Jewish yearning for Jerusalem throughout thousands of
year of exile became a reality. The new mode of emotional exhilaration
was exemplified in the song Jerusalem of Gold composed by
Naomi Shemer a few months before the war. It originally depicted an empty
marketplace in the old city. After the war she changed a verse and
added these words. "We have returned to
the cisterns & the market place. A ram's horn sounds on
the Temple Mount in the Old City". Indeed, patriotic pride,
combined with religious enthusiasm filled the hearts and
minds of many Jews in Israel and abroad following
the unification of Jerusalem. The war also brought
about demographic change. Few Druze villages in
the Golan Heights and few thousand Bedouin in
the Sinai peninsula. But above all, more than two million
Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza Strip were now under
Israeli sovereignty and control. Among them were the majority
of the Palestinian refugees who fled their home in 1948 and
settled in refugees camp. This huge Arab population was
put under military regime and was given a temporary legal status
as population under occupation. Israel had to cope with this new
population under its control which endangered the Jewish
majority of the state and challenged its claim of being a democracy. Early attempts to force them
to move eastward failed. And the government made no
decision concerning the future of the occupied territories and
the fate of the residents. Indeed within Israel,
a major public debate arose over the future of the conquered
territories and their inhabitants. Supporters of the Greater Israel did not
want to concede an inch of the new land. This grassroots movement
had two sub-groups. Religious and Secular. The religious group embrace
a Nationalist-Messianic attitude, and viewed the war's outcome
as a divine intervention. For them, it was a sign that
Messiah was soon to arrive. For these devout believers
settling the entire land of Israel was an obligatory fulfillment
of the holy commanded, a mitzvah. This land is ours, declared
the spiritual leader of the national religious group Rabbi Zvi Yehudah Kook. There are no Arab territories or
Arab lands. Only Israeli land- the land
of our fathers forever, and everything within biblical
boundaries belongs to Israel. Religious followers of
Greater Israel perceived the victory as a step towards the imminent
redemption of the Jewish people. The secular supporter of
the Greater Israel believed that in the wake of the difficult
experience of the Six-Day War, the new territories give Israel more
secured and defensible borders. They also felt that the historic tie with
the Jewish people to the land of Israel justified retaining and even annexing
the territories particularly Jerusalem and Judea and Samaria. On the other hand many Israelis
supported the compromise based on the principle of Land for Peace. Fearing the political and
moral outcome of occupation, they believed that the outcome of
the war provided a good opportunity to negotiate for
peace with the Arab countries. They warned against demographic
implications and the inhereted immorality
in any kind of occupation. Politically, the Israeli government
acknowledged in principle the Land for Peace option and officially
accepted United Nation Resolution 242 with a particular interpretation. Practically, both Israel and
the Arabs refused any diplomatic initiative that was based
on implementing the resolution. The Arab countries signed the Khartoum
Agreement Declaring the three nos. No nagotiation with Israel. No recognition of Israel. And no peace with Israel. The Israeli government made no
decisions regarding the territory. Cabinet minister Yig'al Alon
introduced a plan to concede the densely populated Palestinian
parts of the West Bank to Jordan. And to keep the Jordan Valley in
Israel's hand as a security measure for defensible border. On the other hand,
Defense Minister Moshe Dayan practically institutionalize
the occupation over the Palestinians. He argued that they should
have limited social and cultural autonomy under Israeli rule. Eventually, the Israeli government
did not approve any official plan, although its subsequent actions were
in accordance with Dayan's plan. The occupation became permanent and
did not evoke any significant resistance from the Palestinian
population for the next 20 years. Israel viewed this policy as
an enlightened occupation which even improved the life of
the residents in the territories. Economically, the war lead to a new yet
unstable prosperity in Israel. New construction plans that were
part of the military project brought an economic boom especially in
the building and infrastructure sector. The building of a huge fortification
line on the shore of the Suez Canal provided an economic boost that benefited
new contractors and entrepreneurs. The occupied territories provided
ample supplies of cheap manpower and thus Arab labor contributes to the growth
of many Israeli firms and businesses. A thriving private sector that used and abused this cheap labor emerge
in various economic field. This economic growth led to a rise
of the standard of living for many segments in the Israeli society. Above all, the great victory
turned the army officers and the commanders into national heroes. The public image of the Defense Minister, Moshe Dayan was elevated to
mythical dimensions among Israelis, Jews all over the world and
even international community. The wartime chief of staff
Yitzchak Rabin was appointed the Israeli ambassador to Washington. Generals, Colonels, Admirals,
Pilot, Commander Fighter, with the help of the media,
became real life role models. It seems that Israel underwent
a militaristic euphoria, which infected its politics and
culture in many ways. The Army became the new idol
of the state of Israel and consequently increased its power and size. A sense of an invincible empire formed
the basis for the new Israeli self confidence mixed with some arrogance and
condescending fright. Prime Minister Eshkol died in 1969 and
was replaced by Golda Meir. The Labor Party won the election. The Jewish world supported the country
with money and even with immigration. The USA became Israel's closest ally and
supporter. The Arabs seemed inferior and
incapable of threatening Israel. New borders from the Suez Canal
to the Golan Heights separated Israel from
its hostile neighbors. Most of the biblical promised land
was under Israeli sovereignty and its Arab population seemed calm and
obedient. The government claimed its willingness for peace but refused to take any
step to accomplish this goal. Most politicians acted
under the assumption that the territories would forever
remain under Israel's control. The government, in fact, approved
the policy of encouraging settlement for defense purpose in selected new
territories such as the Golan Heights, the Jordan Valley, and parts of Sinai. Although a war of attrition was taking
place along borders, especially with Egypt, the civilians continued
to celebrate their new well being. The expression,
"our situation has never been better", demonstrates common Israeli
sentiment at that time. Most people agreed with Dayan's
declaration that better to have Sharem a-Sheikh without peace, than to
have peace without Sharem a-Sheikh. Only few critical voices were heard. Among them was that of
the playwright Hanoch Levin. In his play The Bath Queen, he portrayed Israeli society
as corrupted and hedonistic. The play was subsequently removed
from stage under public pressure. Another critic was a scientist and
philosopher, Yeshayahu Leibovitz,
who foresaw gloomy future for Israel if it would continue to hold
such large population under occupation. Even David Ben-Gurion,
who had already retired from politics, expressed his hope that Israel would
give back the occupied territories. However, most of the Israeli ignored,
opposed, and even ridiculed such statements.
