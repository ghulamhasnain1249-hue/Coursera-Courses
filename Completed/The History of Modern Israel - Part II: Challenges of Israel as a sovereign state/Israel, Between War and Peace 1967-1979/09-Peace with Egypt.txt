In the aftermath of the October War, a new equilibrium was established
between Israel and Egypt. After the shocking effect of the war,
Israel, having sustained very heavy losses and
having lost much of the sense of its euphoric invincibility after the 1967 war, Israel was now a lot more willing
to negotiate seriously with Egypt. Egyptians were also fully
aware of their limitations, considering the results of the war even
after such an auspicious beginning when the Egyptians had very successful military
campaigns in the first phase of the war. By the end, the Israelis were
only some 60 miles from Cairo when the cease fire came into effect. Israeli and Egyptian forces on
the Egyptian side of the canal were in very close quarters
when the war came to an end. And because they were in
such very close quarters, there was the constant danger that there
would be a resumption of the hostilities. Thus the two parties came to
an agreement in January 1974 for the separation of their
forces along the Suez Canal. And according to this agreement,
Israeli forces withdrew from the Egyptian side of the canal,
while the Egyptians were allowed to keep a relatively small size military
force on the Israeli side of the canal. And the negotiations also
included the establishment of a buffer zone between the Egyptian
forces and the Israeli forces. And an agreement on
the exchange of prisoners and an agreement by Egypt to work towards
the reopening of the Suez Canal. All these components of the agreement were to make the resumption of war
between the parties far less likely. A similar agreement on
separation of forces was made between Israel and
Syria in May, 1974. Here too, the war had ended with
Israeli forces even deeper in Syrian territory than they had been before
the war, only some 25 miles from Damascus. So Israel agreed to withdraw,
more or less, to the previous line on the Golan Heights,
allowing the Syrians a minor symbolic gain in territory, which made very
little difference on the ground. And the negotiations with the Syrians were
far more difficult, but in the end, like with Egypt, included a buffer zone between
the parties and an exchange of prisoners. It is this agreement from May,
1974 that has lasted for over 40 years in governing
the relationship between Israel and Syria. In September, 1975, a second disengagement agreement was signed between Israel and
Egypt. According to the second
disengagement agreement, Israel withdrew from further territories
in Sinai, establishing essentially a non-belligerancy agreement in which
the parties took a most significant step towards finally ending
the conflict between them. In the new circumstances and
the situation on the ground, with a large UN-patrolled buffer zone between the
Israeli forces and the Egyptian forces, some 20 to 25 miles wide, and the limited
forces of both parties on either side, war between Egypt and
Israel was most unlikely. The agreements of the separation of forces
had been engineered by the step-by-step diplomacy of Henry Kissinger,
the US Secretary of State. Kissinger did not believe that
a comprehensive settlement with all the relevant
parties was really possible. But in early 1977, a new administration
entered the White House. This was the Carter administration, and the Carter administration came with the
new idea of a comprehensive settlement. And the Carter administration
sought to reconvene the International Geneva Conference
as it had been done once in December, 1973 after the October War. The American policy was to reconvene
the International Conference as the stepping stone towards a comprehensive
settlement between Israel and its Arab neighbors in general. But Israel had serious reservations about
the idea of an international conference. From the Israeli point of view, an international conference would isolate
Israel in an impossible negotiation with all of its Arab neighbors at one and
the same time. And therefore,
Israel was not enthusiastic at all about the Carter idea of
an international conference, and this put Israel on a collision course
with the Carter administration. But Carter didn't only differ with Israel. Carter differed also with
the Egyptian President Sadat on the idea of
an international conference, which meant Soviet participation
in this international effort. For Sadat, the idea of an international
conference was a symbolic formality and not the place for
serious bilateral negotiations. Sadat, similar to the Israelis,
feared that a negotiating process in which the Soviet Union and
other Arab players would be involved, would make peace much more difficult
to attain, and not any easier. The US position on comprehensive peace and
the Soviet role was actually closer to the Syrian position than it was to
either the Egyptians or to the Israelis. And the Syrians actually wanted
an international conference and a joint Arab delegation. Not so much to negotiate with Israel,
but to block Sadat and to prevent a separate Egyptian-Israeli
deal which the Syrians feared would create an entirely new balance
of power in the Arab-Israeli conflict, much to the detriment of the Syrians. Another component of US policy in
the International Conference was the US support for a joint Arab delegation
that would include PLO participation. But the PLO, pretty much like the Syrians,
supported the idea of Geneva and an international conference. Less for making peace with Israel than for
bolstering their own position as the sole legitimate representative of
the Palestinians, and to block Jordan from coming to some kind of understanding
or an agreement with Israel. In the meantime, dramatic political
developments took place in Israel. In May, 1977, Menachem Begin,
the head of the right wing Likud party, won the elections in Israel for
the first time ever. And Menachem Begin had his own priorities. That meant peace with Egypt long before
the idea of peace with the Palestinians. For Begin, peace with the Palestinians may
mean having to give up part of greater Israel, and that was not in
Begin's ideological agenda. So Israel and Egypt actually
developed a common interest, where both sides actually were more
interested in an effort to create peace between them than they were
interested in the efforts for an international conference that
neither one of them really believed it. Sadat was anxious to finally achieve
the objects of the October War, and the objectives of the October War were
to create a negotiating process that would eventually lead to Israel's
withdrawal from all of Sinai. Sadat also had domestic pressures
that he had to address. In early 1977,
there were food riots in Egypt. And Egypt was in desperate need for
some kind of political progress that would allow Egypt also to
improve its economic situation. In August, 1977,
Prime Minister Begin of Israel met with the Romanian president, Nicolae Ceausescu. And Begin told Ceausescu of his
desire to make peace with Egypt, and Ceausescu passed
the message on to Sadat. But the main secret
channel between Israel and Egypt was through
the Moroccan King Hassan. And in mid-September, 1977, Moshe Dayan,
the Israeli foreign minister, and Hassan Tuhami, the Egyptian deputy prime
minister, held secret talks in Morocco. But on the first of October,
1977, the United States, together with the Soviet Union,
issued a joint statement calling for an international conference
under joint US and Soviet auspices to negotiate
Arab-Israeli peace. The joint statement by the US and
the Soviet Union caught Israel and Egypt off guard, and
angered both Sadat and Begin. The US was convinced that without the USSR there would be no deal with Syria and
the PLO. Egypt and Israel were convinced
that with the USSR, Syria, and the PLO,
there would be no deal with anyone at all. The time had come for
a bold new departure. And it is against this background that
Sadat came to the very extraordinary conclusion that he should take the brave
and unprecedented move of making a visit to Jerusalem to speak to the Israelis
directly in their own Parliament. And thus, Sadat arrived in
Jerusalem in November, 1977 on his historic groundbreaking visit to Israel,
recognizing Israel and making this extraordinary gesture
of peace with the Jewish state. But when Sadat appeared in the Israeli
Parliament in the Knesset in Jerusalem, in his speech he made it very clear
that he had not come to Israel for a separate deal. There had to also be an agreement not
only between Israel and the Egyptians, but Israel also had to make
concessions to the Palestinians. It was important for Sadat, though driven by Egyptian individual
particular interests, it was important for Sadat not to appear to be abandoning
the Palestinians altogether. And in his speech in the Knesset, Sadat
demanded that there be an independent Palestinian state, that Israel fully
withdraw from the West Bank in Gaza, and that Israel agreed to divide
Jerusalem into an Arab capital of the Palestinian state and
the Jewish part as the capital of Israel. In exchange for his dramatic recognition
of Israel, Sadat expected similar dramatic concessions from the Israeli side,
but this is not exactly what happened. The Israeli cabinet seemed to be divided. Prime Minster Menachem Begin was
more reluctant in his agreement for complete withdrawal of Israel from
the Sinai Peninsula that Egypt demanded than was the foreign minister and the defense minister, Moshe Dayan, and
Ezer Weizman in the Israeli cabinet. And Begin's hesitation and concern for
Israel's long-term security, and therefore reluctance to
commit to complete withdrawal almost drove the initiative to failure. And it was against this possible
failure of this historic initiative that the American president,
Jimmy Carter, convened a conference in
September 1978 of Israel and Egypt and the United States to try and
bring about a final agreement and prevent the failure of
the Sadat initiative to Israel. The US role in achieving
an agreement between Israel and the Egyptians was critical. The parties could accept American ideas
more easily than they could accept them from the other side. And Israel finally agreed to withdraw
fully from the settlements it had established in the Sinai Peninsula, from the airfields that it
had built there as well. And the United States agreed
to rebuild the airfields that Israel had abandoned in Egypt,
in Israeli territory, with considerable US financial and
technical assistance. The United States also promised
an aid package to Egypt. Egypt, in turn,
agreed to supply Israel with oil. And the final treaty was signed in March,
1979, creating full peaceful relations
between Egypt and Israel. The agreement between Israel and
Egypt that was achieved at Camp David did not only relate to
Egyptian-Israeli relations. There was a Palestinian section in the agreement that was achieved
in the Camp David negotiations. And the Palestinian section dealt
with the creation of autonomy for the Palestinians in the West Bank and
Gaza. And it is important to note that
there was no binding linkage between the implementation of
the Palestinian section and the implementation of
the Israeli-Egyptian section. That is, failure to achieve agreement on
the implementation on the Palestinian section did not automatically lead to a
breakdown on the Israeli- Egyptian front. The autonomy talks went nowhere,
mainly because there were no Palestinians who were
really willing to take part in them. After all, autonomy in the West Bank and
Gaza was not sufficient for the PLO or for any of the Palestinians who resided
in the West Bank and Gaza itself. And despite the fact that
the autonomy talks did not take off, the treaty between Israel and Egypt held. What that meant was that Menachem Begin,
Prime Minister of Israel, had essentially succeeded
in trading Sinai for the maintenance of Israeli
control over all of Israel. Peace with Egypt had
great historical meaning. It dramatically altered the regional
balance of power in Israel's favor. Egypt had now finally departed from
the order of battle on the Arab side. What this meant was that the Arab states were not really capable anymore
of waging war against Israel. If Egypt was no longer in
the order of battle, Syria or any other Arab state could not go
to war with Israel without Egypt. And therefore, Egypt at peace with
Israel meant that the Arab states were no longer capable of realistically
thinking in terms of war with Israel. And in fact, since the war of 1973, Israel has not been forced to fight any
further wars with any of the Arab states. Although Israel did not fight
the Arab states anymore, the active conflict with
the Palestinians continued.
