The provisional government became the
permanent one after election were held. Evidently, the labor block
that led the Yishuv from the 1930s, also led the country during these decades. MAPAI, the largest labor block party,
under the leadership of Ben-Gurion, became an enduring hegemonic party. It received 35% to 40% of votes
in all elections that took place during these decades. Since it's never reached 50% of the vote, it formed a coalition
government with other parties. Mostly with the religious block,
the civic block, and more leftist parties
from the labor block. The major opposition
party was the revisionist known as the right-wing Herut party
under the leadership of Menachem Begin. The first president of
Israel was Chaim Weizmann, the veteran leader of
the World Zionist Movement. While formally Israel operated
as a western democracy. Some may argue that it lacks certain
features of liberal democracy like we can find in western states. First, despite written promise in
its Declaration of Independence, there is no constitution
in Israel until today. Without a constitution that regulates
balance of power between the branches of the government, the legal system
is very amorphous and unstable. Without a constitution, there are no basic rule to secure human
rights and equality before the law. Without a constitution, the government in power can employ
extreme measure against its opponents. It can operate under emergency decrees and
regulation. And in fact the mandatory emergency
rule was never abolished in Israel. And still provide the government with the
ability to control without restriction. Second, Israel imposed a military
regime on most of its Arab population that lives in rural areas. The country still view the Arab
citizens as a potential enemy, and thus restricted their ability to function
de facto as equal citizens in many ways. The military regime was perceived for years as necessary evil that helped
to cope with the Arab residents of the state that were still viewed
as fifth column by many Israelis. This feeling gradually subsided, and the military regime was
finally abolished in 1966. Third, the long political control of MAPAI shaped the nature of the civil and
military service of the new country. Most top officials during the 50s and
60s were affiliated with the party, so national goals
intermingled with party interests. Some would claim that it
was necessary measure that enable to set the basis for
the state. Others would criticize this
phenomenon as non-democratic. The media was free, but the government
had the ability to exercise censorship. Many ideological newspaper
flourished in this decade but only one official national radio
broadcast was able to operate. Fourth, the constant
preoccupation with defense and security issues created a military
atmosphere that permeated Israeli culture. Some may argue that such militarism
prevented Israel from being a true liberal and civil society. While others view it as
a form of healthy patriotism, without real effect on the democratic
nature of the new state.
