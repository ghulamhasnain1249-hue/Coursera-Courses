Israel as a sovereign state for
the Jews enacted the law of return. Which granted automatic citizenship
to any Jew who wished to come and settle in the country. The state's declared goal was to help
as many Jews as possible to immigrate. Indeed, between 1949 and 1955, Jewish population in Israel
almost tripled in number. The mass immigration brought together
many Jews from different countries and culture, speaking diverse languages. And having various religious,
ritual, tradition, and customs. Many were Holocaust survivors. But the majority came from the Islamic
countries of the Middle East and north Africa. Over 1 million immigrants, most of
them refugees and without any property were taken in by Israel's 650,000
residents in a very short period of time. These displaced and
uprooted immigrants moved to a country with weak economy lacking housing and
security. This was an extraordinary undertaking
by the society and its institution, which made a tremendous effort
to absorb such mass immigration. The immigrants were housed in makeshift
camps on abandoned British army bases. And in home of Palestinians
who had not been permitted to return after the War of Independence. The conditions in the camps were horrific. As the fledgling state found it difficult
to provide sufficient food, clothing, housing, and sanitation service for
the new camp. Later on, the government
constructed Ma'abarot, miserable temporary neighborhoods, comprises of
shanties and tents put on near cities. Here too, the plight of
the immigrants did not improve. And they felt neglected and resentful when exposed to the relatively
superior situation of the veterans. Many of them lost any sense of dignity and very few were lucky to find
manual low paying jobs. Officially, Israel did much to
bring Jews from around the world, and to facilitate their move. In certain countries this
required bribing governments and signing arm deals which was
a great financial undertaking. Practically however,
at the beginning of the 1950s, The young country almost collapsed. In 1951, immigration was restricted, and only immigrants who were at risk
in their countries of origin or could arrive at their own
expense were allowed in. This selective immigration which lasted
until 1954 was harshly criticized. But in retrospect it appears
an unavoidable strategy. The wide scale absorption that
took place in Israel in the 50s proceeded despite enormous difficulties. Most veteran citizens that
managed the absorption effort came from European background and culture. Consequently, it was very difficult for the immigrants from Islamic countries to
integrate into such an unfamiliar culture. Moreover, the authorities tried,
sometimes forcibly, to impose a new Israeli identity on the immigrants
while rejecting their heritage and tradition. The guiding ideology was that of
the melting pot, which tried to forge a common new homogenous Israeli
identity out of diverse backgrounds. In practice the European Sabre
identity was seen as the norm and the immigrants were expected
to adjust themselves to it. The oriental traditions and
religious rituals of the new immigrants were ignored and
perceived as inferior and primitive.
