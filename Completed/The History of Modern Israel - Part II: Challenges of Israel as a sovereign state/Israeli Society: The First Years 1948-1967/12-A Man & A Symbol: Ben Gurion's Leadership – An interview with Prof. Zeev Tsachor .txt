Joining us today is
Professor Zeev Tzahor from the Ben Gurion University
in the Negev (Southern Israel), who also served as Ben Gurion's
last personal assistant. Hello, Zeev.
-Hello. Ben Gurion has the image
of a decisive leader who understood
the challenges facing him. Does this image truly comply
with his personality and character? Ben Gurion is an exceptional phenomenon
in the history of leadership in general. When he was 16 years old, during the transition
between the 19th and 20th centuries, he decides to be the bearer
of good tidings for the Jewish people. Of course, he never actually said it,
but he acted like a leader-to-be. As an example: from that age,
he wrote a journal and saved it
for his entire lifetime. He documented every letter that
he wrote and everything that he did. Thus, he expressed his readiness
to redeem the Jewish people, and I specifically use
his terminology, "redemption..." Remember, we're talking about a most
intensive period in Jewish history. If we review the period between
1900 until 1963, when he retired, (he then went on to live
an additional 10 years of active life) the history of the Jews, of Zionism
and especially of the State of Israel with everything leading to its creation
and formation, are so compressed. Of course, in history,
there is no way to quantify it, but it would be hundreds of years
of history under normal conditions and Ben Gurion is always located
in the place in which he can observe, check, write down, document,
and finally bear witness. Ben Gurion wrote several books
from his point of view, of course, about the history
of the Jewish people. And all this was done
emphatically, with resolve though he didn't speak about a State. He spoke about the way to a State
and was one of the founders of the Pre-State. Was he truly so determined in the numerous
cross-roads under his leadership? Yes, but there are a few errors
in the perception of his character. He is determined.
He is tenacious. He also presents himself as
a tough, determined person, also in his character, his
tone of voice and articulation, but... not everyone knows, but in a few
crucial cross-roads of his life, he understood that he must
switch his stance or ideology sometimes to the exact opposite
of what he was before. I'll give you an example
from the days of Pre-State Israel. He begins
his important public position as the secretary-general of the
Histadrut (Federation of Labor) which became the foundation of the Palmach,
the Hagana, Solel Boneh, Tnuva, Mashbir, all those institutions that were
so important to Pre-State Israel. He began his role
with the idea of the class struggle. For those who know the history
of European socialism and the Internationale... He says,
"I am a Bolshevik." His exact words. He quickly learns
that Zionism cannot be built without the help
of the bourgeoisie. So he makes a pact with Haim Weizman,
the president of the Zionist Movement which I call, in one of my articles,
"A pact of enmity." They really didn't like one another,
but they needed each other. That's a simple example of a person
who changes direction completely and calls his book:
"From Class to Nation." No longer a class struggle, but rather,
the nation is the class. -Interesting. What is the impact
of David Ben Gurion's role, if any, on the modern history
of Israel? As those who research history,
we often debate the question of the role of public figures
in history and we tend to say,
with subtle Marxist undertones, that history is determined by
a series of economical processes. But we know that at times,
an individual makes his mark on history
and makes an impact on it. With Ben Gurion, at least once,
the drama lies with a single individual. For example?
-On May 12, 1948, and note that the State of Israel
was founded on May 14th... Just two days earlier,
the People's Administration gathers. That temporary government had to
decide if to establish the State or not. The American Secretary of State,
Marshal, an admirable man, who knew a thing or two
about the army and war, says to Moshe Shertok,
as he was called then... (Two days later, he'd become
Moshe Sharet, the Foreign Minister.) He says, "We predict the length of time
that Israel will manage to survive "the invasion..." of 7 Arab States,
as they called it then, "would be up to two or three weeks." This is what Shertok brings to the
meeting that evening. He flew in and went straight to the meeting
to share it with the people. Knees began to tremble.
And rightfully so, I would say. -Yes. 10 people, The People's
Administration, the temporary government. Four had made up their minds.
"We oppose." It's only been three years since the
Jewish people suffered the Holocaust and nothing helped,
so now, with seven Arab nations, the memory
of the Holocaust is still... -Strong. There is one person who is absolutely
sure he is in favor. Ben Gurion. Five are undecided. The meeting goes on for 13 hours. One of those present remarked...
His name was Mordechai Bentov. (He was the one who came up
with the idea of a bi-national state.) He said, "Ben Gurion aimed
the gun of history at my skull. "I could not refuse him
and I decided to vote in favor." And so, the other four
followed suit in the dramatic vote. Perhaps the most important vote
in our history. Six vs. four decided on the
establishment of the State of Israel. We'll never know what might have happened
if one would have switched sides but it is clear to everyone
and to all the participants back then that Ben Gurion was the one
that set the process in motion and that is where the establishment
of the State was decided. -Interesting. In conclusion, as one who saw him
in his golden years, could you pinpoint the traits that characterized him
as an individual? Yes, but it isn't so simple. A young man of 16 who sets forth
to become a leader and immediately assumes
all the responsibilities of a leader: At the age of 18 he is
a central figure at Poalei Zion, at the age of 20 he was a prominent figure,
soon after - secretary of the Histadrut, the administrative director
of the Sochnut, and the Prime Minister in the most
formative, critical first years. So he assumes the single-mindedness of a man
who only makes history. For instance, he didn't know
how to raise a family. That's pretty mundane, because
he travelled all around the world and his daily schedule
was just amazing. From morning till night. I don't know
where he got his strength. It was so all-encompassing,
he had a hard time making conversation,
what we call "small talk." In his old age, when he already lived
alone in Sdeh Boker, Sometimes people would come to visit.
They would bring a photographer... And he knew he had to host them. He didn't always have
what to say to them, especially, I'm sorry to say,
he wasn't much of a feminist: "With men, you can talk politics.
But what can I say to the women?" So he adopted two questions. One was: "What is your surname?" And he'd develop that into...
He was an expert in names. That would give him 5-7 minutes
with the photographer. The second question was:
"How many children do you have?" If two, then why not three?
Three, then why not two? And then he'd say:
"I have three children. "I really wanted four."
But Paula, his wife, didn't. Now just think how amazed
that woman would feel as she left, and usually, she would be an important
woman, the wife of a millionaire. "Ben Gurion told me such intimate,
confidential things." She'd just be bursting with pride
and excitement. She never knew
that he'd asked the same questions to thousands of people.
-He asked the same question. But that was also the way
he expressed his political prowess. He was a great politician. Thank you, Zeev Tzahor.
-Thank you. -It was very interesting.
