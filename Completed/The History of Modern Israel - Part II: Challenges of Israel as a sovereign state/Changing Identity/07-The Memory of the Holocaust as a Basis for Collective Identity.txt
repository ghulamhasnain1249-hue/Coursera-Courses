Israeli society is diverse and
multicultural. The Zionis narrative is under
attack from opposite directions. Despite its political marginality, post Zionism seems as an existential
position for many Israelis. Religious Zionism enchanted many
Israelis on the other hand. Therefore, it's becoming increasingly
hard to find a common denominator around which Israeli collective identity
can be shaped. in the last generation it seems that among a Jewish
majority of Israeli citizens. The memory of the Holocaust
constitutes such a shared basis. The Holocaust has an enormous
role in constructing Jewish identity in the last generations. Obviously, from the beginning, the Holocaust awarness play a significant
role in Israeli memorial culture. But Israel's attitude towards such a
trauma is complex, dynamic and ambivalent. From the creation of the state,
the Holocaust was present in daily life, political rhetoric and
state sermons. The Holocaust became a force
of legitimately for the state, both domestically and
internationally and was perceived as the undeniable evidence that
justifies the Zionist stance and the two major aspects,
the Negation of the Diaspora, and state sovereignty as a solution. Israeli society's response to
the Holocaust in the first decade was a mixture of deep shock at
the dimensions of the catastrophe and a sense of anger towards the victims and survivors that did not fight back and
went like sheep to the slaughter. The Israelis commemorate the individual
with whom they were able to identify, those who fought against the Nazis,
the partisans and especially the fighter of
the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. Holocaust Martyrs' and  Heroes'
Remembrance day was established in the calendar on the date that
the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising had erupted. The leaders of the uprising were portrayed
as part of the long lineage of Jewish fighters over history, and in line with
the heroes of the war of independence. The rest of the survivors
were neglected and ignored. The Holocaust was tolerable
as a public memory, but difficult to bear as a private memory. "The Holocaust was bigger than life,"
wrote historian Anita Shapira. It was history, destiny,
a past that speaks to the future. Yet, it was not ours. It did not require our acquaintance,
understanding, and internalization. This remote attitude was further amplified
by Holocaust survivors themselves. Who, in their tortured yearning to
assimilate into Israeli society, imposed upon themselves a mute restraint. Even their children hardly dared
to ask them about their past. This attitude started to change
with the Eichmann trial in 1961, when Holocaust survivors
called to testify in Court. They told their stories for
the first time. For weeks, the trial was
the center of public attention. After it ended, the Holocaust could no
longer be dismissed as a private memory. In the following decades,
the Israeli general public grew increasingly willing to listen to
the memories of the survivors. Since the mid 80s,
the Holocaust has turned into a central component of memorial culture in Israel. State law of education was
amended to include Holocaust awareness as a major
educational objective. A total of 30 hours,
which constitutes one-third of yearly academic unit in history,
were allocated for this purpose. It was explained that beyond cognitive
understanding, the holocaust must be emotionally experienced by all
Israelis, especially the young generation. Starting in the late 80s
school delegations have been participating in a journey
to the death camp in Poland. Since then,
these journeys have become a central rite of passage for hundreds of
thousands of Israeli youngsters. Since 2000, the Israeli army has
been sending many delegates to Poland under the project
named witness in uniform. These informal tools proved
to be very effective both in integrating the Holocaust  as a central
component in the collective memory, and in using this memory to reinforce and
justify the Zionist ideology. However, this effectiveness
proved to be a double edge sword when we see the Holocaust
memory being abused and manipulated for political purposes. The debates over teaching the Holocaust were always overshadowed by
the division between right and left, with each side using the catastrophe
differently, for its own needs. The right wing sees the Holocaust, the tragic proof of the fate
of Jewish existence. And frames it in the familiar mode of a war against the ever
existing enemies of Israel. Whether the enemy is Nazi, or Arab, or Muslim,
the enemy had to be exterminated. The anxiety of the left in
contrast was caused specifically by the experience of ruling
over Arab population, and the fear that Israeli soldiers would
come to resemble brutal Nazi soldiers. It also became an instrument for Israeli liberals, whose representatives
in various fields sought to emphasis the universal democratic
dimension of the Holocaust memory. In order to prevent the emergence of
racism and fanaticism in Israeli society. Thus, the integration of the Holocaust
in the polarized Israeli experience, no longer as a founding trauma alone,
but increasingly as a memory subject to conflicting interpretation and
manipulative political uses, has made it a problematic
unresolved issue even today.
