As the negation of exile was central
pillar of the Zionist ideology, Zionists believe that Jewish people
had no future in the diaspora. Therefore the state of Israel
views itself as the only perennial home and safe refuge for Jews. Yet, a closer look at
the relationship between Israel and the Jewish Diaspora in
the 21st century reveals that about half of the world
Jewry lives outside Israel. This phenomenon challenges basic
Zionist assumptions making the relationship between Israel and
the Diaspora quite complex. With the establishment of Israel, the Jewish Agency became the central
institution responsible for fostering relations between the state and
the Jews in the diaspora. It was in charge of encouraging
immigration fundraising among Jewish communities abroad and maintaining
the link to the state of Israel. The holocaust in Europe, the actual
disappearance of Jewish centers in Islamic countries, and a significant
decline in Jewish life in Russia moved the core of Jewish life to
the democratic Western countries. Today, about 5 million Jews lived in
North America, most of them in the US, close to 2 million in western Europe and
South America. And about half a million live
in Australia, Asia, and Africa. Already in the 50s Ben-Gurion, against
his will, signed the Blaustein documents, accepting the fact that the American Jews
are first and foremost American citizens. They have historic and
spiritual ties to Israel. But these ties are not at the expense
of their loyalty to America. Thus Israel acknowledged the legitimate existence of the Jewish
communities in North America. This document reflects
the relationship between Israel, which view itself as
the potential home for all Jewish people on one hand, and
Jews in the west, who regard themselves, first and foremost, as citizens of
the country in which they live. Even the vast majority of Jewish
activists who view themselves as Zionists support Israel
either through donation and investment, or
by backup a pro-Israeli political lobby. Most of them never seriously
considered moving to Israel. This situation raises
challenging questions. Is Israel capable of being the spiritual
center of all the Jewish people? Is there a need for such a center? Is it possible that most Jews gradually
assimilated into the affluent society of the West, and only small ultra-orthodox
portion will endure over time? Is Israel a guarantee
against modern antisemitism, or is it a new generator of antisemitism
disguised as anti-Israeli position? Can we expect a rise in Jewish
immigration to Israel in the future as a consequence of unpredictable events? Do the prosperous and secure Jewish
community at the diaspora attract and encourage Israelis to leave their state
and to emigrate back to the diaspora? Can the national center in Israel and
the open and liberal Jewish world at the diaspora find
ways to foster common Jewish identity, Or are they destined to depart
toward separate ways? The most practical question
relating to Israel's relations with the Jewish communities in the
diaspora is the degree of influence they have over Israelis' politics and
the identity crisis that is a result of Israel's controversial
policies toward the Palestinians. Today, Israel is becoming
more of a divisive, than a uniting element among
Jewish communities abroad. Especially among American Jewry
which is facing unprecedented crisis over the question of
continuing support to Israel as long as it maintains its policy
toward the Palestinians. There are no simple
answers to this question. Yet as for
today we can detect four approaches of the Jewish world towards Israel. First, identification,
some Jews in the diaspora completely identify with Israel and
its policies. Few of them ultimately
immigrate to the country. Most of them assert their support
through donations, frequent visits and vigorous undisputable,
even automatic support of Israel, regardless of its governmental policies. These Jews call themselves Zionists. For them, national sovereignty is the
ultimate solution for the Jewish people. Their goal is to strengthen this solution
despite the fact that they themselves, for various reasons,
probably will not move to Israel. Second is ambiguity. For many Jews,
the existence of Israel is meaningful. Precisely because of that, they allow
themselves frequently to criticize the policy of Israeli fovernment. They donate money to Israeli or
Jewish organizations and NGOs that relate to their opinions,
and avoid donating money to others. While taking seriously
their Jewish identity, these people don't automatically
support Israeli policies when they feel it contradicts central Jewish
values of humanism and social justice. Third, confrontation, some Jews in the
diaspora vehemently oppose Israeli policy, and few of them even deny its
right to exist as a Jewish state. A few of them even claim that the Jewish agenda can only be
developed in the diaspora. Occasionally they join anti-Zionist,
pro-Palestinian movement. And ironically, two extremes
typically make up this group of anti-Zionist Jews,
the liberal extreme left who view the Jewish state as
a form of racist nationalism. And the ultra-orthodox Jewish
enclave that traditionally oppose the Zionist sin of
promoting the Jewish redemption. Fourth, indifference,
many young Jews in the West don't identify themselves as part of the Jewish world
in their culture and way of life. Consequently, they don't invest
any meaning in the creation and survival of the state of Israel. In a process of assimilation some of
them are altogether forsaking the Jewish identity. Others, while keeping their
Jewish identity and heritage, don't see Israel as being
an integral part of it. They can be active in local Jewish
organizations, attend Jewish service, support Jewish study centers, but will
exclude Israel from the Jewish activities. They will never express interest in
the fate of the country that claim to be their ancient homeland or
potential future refuge. There is a dispute among Jews which one of
these four attitudes is the dominant one. Dominance has also changed over time. After the victory in the Six Days War, most Jews demonstrated utter solidarity
and enthusiasm toward Israel. Today, such an attitude is diminishing,
especially among young American Jews. There is a strong and powerful pro-Israeli lobby of Jewish
rich establishment in America. But its role is controversial
among many other American Jews. In general, for many Jews in the diaspora, the existence of a safe and
sound Jewish state is important. It may serve as an assurance
against anti-Semitism and even as a potential
refuge in time of need. But the link between Israel and Jews in the West also entails tensions and
difficulties. The link between Jews and
Israel may sometimes lead to confounding resistance to
Israel with antisemitism, a stance often propagated by
radical Islamists in the West. Thus some Jews claim that
Israel's controversial policy provoked antisemitism and
damaged world Jewry. It seems that the tension between
the universal cosmopolitan dimension of Judaism and
its particular national dimension characterize the long
history of Jewish people. These tensions illustrate today
the complicated relationship between Israel as the state of the Jews and
the Jewish diaspora, thereby challenging the Zionist
notion of negation of exile.
