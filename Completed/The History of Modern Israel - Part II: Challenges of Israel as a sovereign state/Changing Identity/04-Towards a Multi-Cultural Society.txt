Despite enormous difficulties,
the Zionist movement succeeded in establishing
the state of Israel. It also aspired to build a new society
that would mold the multitude components and characteristics of Jewish identities
in exile into one Israeli identity. A melting pot policy aimed to create
an Israeli identity of the new Jew. However, from the late 70s
it became apparent that such a mission of human
engineering fell short of reality. In an ever-changing political, social, and cultural situation, and
within the dynamic context of history, the Israeli society today seems
diverse and multifaceted. Some feared that this process would weaken
the unity of the Israeli society and define it as split and segmentation. Others praise it as a healthy and normal liberation from the illusion
of homogenous identity that is the result of a coercive and
fake attempt at unification. Anyway, in its current state, like so
many other societies around the globe, the Israeli society reflects
a variety communities and identities. Men and women, veterans,
and newcomers, Jews and Arabs, secular and religious leaders,
center and periphery in mosaic of diverse and
innumerable culture. Amos Oz, one of the leading
Israel authors and intellectuals wrote that the culture tend to flourish
precisely in places of contradictions, tensions, incongruities,
and even weirdness. But whoever wants to accomplish
his dreams should know that the price of accomplishment
is a compromise. Compromise with reality,
with human constrains and limitation, with the other from outside,
and the other from within. Not everyone in Israel acknowledged
the need to compromise. Therefore, the multicultural reality
of Israeli society did not gain yet a normative status, and occasionally,
provoke hostility and resentment. The late Israeli sociologist,
Baruch Kimmerling, albeit generalizing, roughly defined six major
groups that make up the Israeli society. Ashkenazi veteran hegemony, educated, mostly secular upper middle class group
of Ashkenazi Jews of european descent. This group resides primarily in
the Tel Aviv metropolitan area and in veteran Kibbutzim and Moshavim. It still holds most of the country's
economic resources and controls elite cultural capital however
It is losing significant political power. This group is shrinking demographically. And despite its professional,
educational, and economic advantages, many within this group feels more and
more alienated from the direction which the Israeli society
politics are heading towards. The traditional Sephardi group
of Jews descendant of immigrants who came to Israel from the Muslim world they predominantly belong
to the lower middle class. This group increased in number and
political power, but still, resent the Ashkenazi establishment,
for what they perceive ongoing historical deprivation and
discrimination against them. Some of them left the ethnic enclaves and
intermixed with the Ashkenazi group. Other remained in the periphery
of development town and marginal neighborhood in the city and in
the rural villages where their parents or grandparents were housed by Israeli
government upon arrival in the 50s. Many of them belong to the rank and file of the Likud party that predominates
Israeli politics since the 80s. A national religious group that
is demographically growing, and whose stronghold is in the settlements, with wide support from religious
Ashkenazi middle class. This group perceives itself as
the new spearhead of Zionism, based on national religious patriotism. Many of them lived in Jerusalem and in area beyond the green line of Judea and
Samaria. In the last generation they increased
their numbers in combative and elite units of the Israeli Army and Ultra Orthodox Haredi
demographically growing group. Originally anti-Zionist that became tribal
and traditional in its orientation. This group abides to its Orthodox leaders,
follows Jewish Orthodox law rather than the Israeli legal system,
do not serve in the army. And in general, isolates itself from
the rest of the society through separate educational and
cultural institutions. It is divided by ethnic origin and
religious affiliation and in general practice claim
pre modern mode of living. They have huge families,
many of them live in poverty depending on contributions from abroad and
on the state welfare. In the last generation,
some of them intermixed, with the national religious group. A large group of Russian immigrants,
that arrived during the 90s. Many of them still live in
cultural enclaves of their own. Many of its members are not considered
Jews according to the Jewish religious law. Which causes many tensions in
a state where religious authority govern many aspect of civic lives
such as marriage and burial. More than 20% of Israeli citizens are Arab they live in their own subculture
in their villages and towns. Most of them Muslims, but
some are Christians and Druze. Most of them view themselves
are national minority and victims of the Zionist achievement. There is hardly any personal integration
between this group and the Jews. Most of them identified as Palestinians,
and feel close affinity to
the Palestinians in the territories. They have their separate
system of education, speak Arabic as first languages,
yet they also speak Hebrew. They regard themselves as
Israelis by citizenship, Palestinians by nationalities,
and Muslims by religion. Striving for internal autonomy,
as well as equal rights, they suffer from traumatic
history of discrimination, expulsion, negligence and
land confiscation. And disinheritance by the Israeli State. They gradually integrate into
Israeli Society and prefer to live as a national minority in Israel hoping to
turn it into a state of all its citizens. To these six groups, we can also add
the unique community of Ethiopians, the Bedouins who constitute
a specific sector of Arab society. And labor immigrants from Asia,
Africa, and South America, the labor immigrants are not
citizens of the state. Some of them have work permits,
others are illegal aliens. Who run for their life as refuge seekers
or looking for jobs and survival while fleeing from their home country due to
hunger, persecutions and other tragedies. To these groups we must add more than
3 million Palestinians living in the territories under the fragile
political control of the Palestinian authority without citizenship and
under Israeli occupation. Indeed, the diverse profile of Israeli
society does not resemble the new Hebrew Sabra that was envisioned by the founding
father of the country. The Israeli societies comprise of various
ethnicities, traditions and sub culture. The question is whether
the Israeli society can engage in a fruitful dialogue between its
segments to achieve a common denominator that will allow
pluralistic existence. Or whether each segment will
dissociate itself from the other, thus, living within its
exclusive identity, boundaries, and
apart from the common public sphere. It is hard to predict the future
direction of Israeli identity. And it is quite obvious that the general
trend is from a homogeneous ideal to a diverse reality and
from a melting pot to multiculturalism.
