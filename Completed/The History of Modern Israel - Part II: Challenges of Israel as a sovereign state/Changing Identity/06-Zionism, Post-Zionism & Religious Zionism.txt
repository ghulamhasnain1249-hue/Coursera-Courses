The Zionist narrative serves as an
overarching historical narrative that for many Israelis explains and
legitimizes the establishment and the enduring existence of the state. This narrative interprets the creation and development of Israel as
a realization of a modern humanistic, as well as national
revolutionary movement, that motivated Jews to return
from exile to sovereignty. Yet since the 90s,
this narrative has been losing its meaning to many sectors of
the Israeli society, and is being challenged from
diametrically opposed direction. Secular post-Zionism on one hand and religious Messianic Zionism on the other. The main criticism of
post-Zionism is on the oxymoron of a Jewish and
democratic state. It claims that if Israel
aspire to be truly democratic, it should issue full equality
to all its citizens. According to the philosopher Joseph Dan,
post-Zionism means Israeli nationalism based
on territorial minimalism, without any specific social or
moral aspiration, without any significant
religious dimension, without the adoption of
a traditional culture, without a scatological foundation, without a deep-rooted Hebrew language
that draws upon its origins. Post-Zionism aims to deepen
the normalization stature of Israel among other nations and
aspires for spiritual integration from a position
of equality with other cultures. Post-Zionist thinking is influenced
by post-nationalist theories that regard any national
community as an imagined community. According to this approach, Zionism invented a Jewish
unity that never existed. In the wake of this criticism, some
post-Zionists utterly reject nationalism, hoping instead to foster
a democratic civil alternative that will ultimately
transform Israeli identity. Post-Zionist sociologist criticized the
melting pot policy as anti-democratic and destructive, which strove to establish
a male, Askhenazi, veteran hegemony. In that process, other groups were
coerced to renounce their culture and sense of identity,
thus were pushed to the physical and cultural margins of the society. Post-Zionist historians reexamine
traditional historiography of the Zionist movement and
the state of Israel. They argue that Zionist historiography
was ideologically biased, aimed at justifying
the existence of Israel, and concealing the unjust
treatment of the Palestinians. Post-Zionist filmmaker,
poets, playwrights and other artists produced various
works that criticized and even ridiculed major Zionist
assumptions and aspirations. So far, post-Zionism as a cultural
intellectual expression is limited in its social and political scope. Its impact can be mainly found
among circles of academics, journalist, and artist working
within elitist creative group that concentrated around what
is called the Tel Aviv bubble. Their political influence is marginal and
perceived as a cultural subversion and even betrayal against the ideological
foundations of the state of Israel. On the other side of the spectrum,
emerges the Messianic religious Zionist narrative
that challenges the modern, humanistic and
western elements of the Zionist story. This narrative views
the Zionist endeavor and the creation of Israel as
the first step in a divine process leading to the creation of
the Kingdom of Israel and the erection of the third temple. According to this narrative the state
of Israel is processing towards the ultimate state of redemption
of the Jewish people. Religious Zionists argue
that although secular modern Zionists were those who initiated
the process and paved the way, they lost their motivation and
vision due to western influence, materialism, fatigue, and individualism. The national religious forces
empowered with belief of the imminent coming of the Messiah became the force
that is taking responsibility over the continuation of the Zionist project. Their aim is to prepare the hearts and
minds of Israeli Jews to see the mission and to change
the identity from secular imitator of the west into authentic
descendants of ancient Hebrews who would carry the mission of Jewish
redemption in the promised land of Israel. Emuna Elon, a public advocate
of the settlement movement, expressed the political implications of
such new narrative on the Zionist mission. She stated that if the people of Israel
have a right to the land of Israel, a historical right, a moral right,
a divine right, then it is all right for them to take it from those who are living
on it, even against their will. Even by force, if the land is ours,
if out of it we were taken and to it we shall return, and if without
it we have no life as a nation or as individuals, then,
we have no choice but to fight for it when we must, and
drive from it whomever we have to. Indeed, this Messianic narrative
stresses the divine nature of the revival of Israel as a Jewish
state in the promised land. In religious schools,
youth movement, synagogues and cultural centers, such a message
is very common and inspiring. Even among fundamental Christian circles,
the state of Israel is perceived in a scatological terms
as the first step in the process that would ultimately lead
to a Messianic event. It is hard to know how strong and effective such a religious Messianic
Zionist narrative is in Israel today. Yet, among many members of
the settlement movement, national religious circles all
around the country, many youngsters that are influenced by fundamentals,
Rabbis, and religious figures. Such a vision provides transcendental
significance to the very existence and further development of Israel. They work hard to turn the country
from a state of the Jews to a Jewish state that should adopt
the religious Jewish laws and culture as its basic mode of conduct and
as a source of common identity. Unlike post-Zionists, the religious Messianic Zionists
are very active politically. They influence right-wing parties and
movements. And lately, even increased their bearing
on the state institution such as absorption centers for newcomers, the
state schools, and even the Israeli Army.
