The vast majority of Israelis
have either European, Near Eastern, or North African origins. Yet, despite this demography,
at least since the late 60s, Israeli society has been looking
towards the United States of America as its role model and
a source of inspiration. Obviously, the US is Israel's
great political ally and supporter since the 60s. Moreover, American Jews, the largest
Jewish community outside of Israel, played a significant role in
strengthening the relationship and affiliation between Israel and the USA. However, the identification with America
goes beyond political interest and became a distinct characteristic
of Israeli collective identity. How can we explain such a phenomenon? Part of the explanation is by default. Europe could not furnish such
an inspirational model because of the historical relationship between
Jews and Gentiles on its soil. Antisemitism, persecution, and discrimination characterized
Jewish life in Europe. It ultimately led to the annihilation
of European Jewry in the Holocaust, thus excluding Europe from being any
normative model for Israeli society. The Arab world of the Middle East
appeared as the very antithesis of Israeli society, and the direct opposite
of what the State of Israel stands for. Most Israeli citizens perceive
the Arab world as a primitive, pre-modern, autocratic example
of the traditional Levant, contradicting Israel's self-image
as a Western island of democracy and modernity in the middle of a hostile area. Thus, from a Zionist perspective,
only the United States shown as a Western, modern and democratic place,
void of any complicate and hostile relationship to the Jews or
to the Jewish State. But beyond default however, there were other cultural components
that made American experience so meaningful to the formation and
transformation of the Israeli society. Both societies developed free from
the constraints of an old regime. They emerged as democracies and republics without having to fight or
overcome kings and nobility. Lack of tradition and
the emphasis on creating something new evoked an iconoclastic
spirit against conventions, traditions and customs of the bourgeois
mentality in both the US and Israel. Many Israelis view the lack
of fixed boundaries and the constant expansion of US territory
as a sign dynamic and healthy development. Thus they positively interpreted
the lack of determined and accepted borders in Israel. The American frontier became an inspired
ideal in Israeli geographical development. The short history of Zionism as
a recent movement was compared to the short history of America,
in contrast to long Jewish history and the thick and heavy European history. In many channels of mass communication,
like the electronic media, entertainment, magazines and journals, the analogy to the American experience
surpasses any other comparison. For example, when Rabin was assassinated,
the media and the press compared the event to
the assassination of Lincoln or Kennedy. Even Walt Whitman's memorial poem for
Lincoln, O, Captain!, My Captain!, was translated into Hebrew, and was
featured in the many memorial ceremonies. Israel as a state of immigrants,
looked for a model and normative guidance by the US
as the most notable and successful immigrant state on earth. The melting pot ideal that presumably
erased old affiliations among immigrants and created a new American
nation was perceived as an inspired ideal to the state that absorbed
Jews from all over the world. In sum, lack of an old regime to overcome,
unclear border, short history and
an immigrant society appealed as the common characteristics of
both countries which accounted for the American influence on
Israeli society and culture. On the other hand,
contradictions between the American and the Israeli experience constituted
another form of relation. Israel is so small. America is so big. Israel is a place surrounded by enemies. America, by oceans and friendly states. Israel is a difficult place to live. America is a paradise of comfort and
prosperity. In Israel, the intensity of the news
gives the impression that the public and private life are intertwined. In contrast,
most Americans live in some inner privacy. Business, sport and
weather dominate the news. While coverage of public
sphere is very limited. For many Israelis, therefore, America
is perceived as an aspired model for a normal, secured, and comfortable life. A fantasy of hope for reaching
the light at the end of the tunnel. This feeling is strengthened
by several internal developments within the Israeli society. Politically, Israel abandoned
the premature stage of ideological party system, and is proceeding toward
an American-style two block system, with minimum ideological differences
between those parties striving for power. Economically, while starting
with a state-controlled economy, Israel is moving fast on the track
to embrace privatization, the American version of modern capitalism,
and thereby reducing both the welfare
state and the power of labor union. Israel is gradually substituting
its egalitarian ethos for an individualistic ethos
based on free enterprise, profit incentive, and consumerism. To many outside observers,
Israel seems, in many ways, more American than many
European countries. It imitates American fashion,
media, popular culture, sports, entertainment, hero worship, youth culture,
as well as economy and politics. Looking at the American standard
of living as a normative model. Many Israelis openly admire
the American way of life, striving to achieve American success
defined in materialistic terms. Others envy the American pluralism,
tolerance, and service awareness. In the last decade or so,
Israel takes pride in developing an American
style high tech industry. with a steady increase in efficiency and performance indicators in a highly
competitive corporate environment. Methods of management and
banking systems, electronic media, and new tabloid journalism are among the
most prominent examples for the process of Americanization that the country
has been undergoing in recent years.
