Following 15 years of largely
uninterrupted Likud hegemony, Labor Party, under the leadership or Yitzhak Rabin,
narrowly won the election in 1992. This victory aroused great expectations,
but also evoked great opposition. It sparked hope for renewal of the peace process and
progress towards solution with the Arab. It also increased fears and
great concern about territorial concessions that will endanger
Israel's very existence. Rabin's government declared its goal for
changing national priority. Large budget were channeled to education, closing gaps between Jewish and
Arab-Israeli sector and to absorbing the large waves of
immigrants from the former Soviet Union. Rabin's government, embarked upon a peace
process expecting to improve Israel's domestic situation together, with
its regional and international status. Exploring the Palestinian, the Syrian and
the Jordanian channels, the government made effort to engage in discussions
between Israel and its enemies. Failure with Syria,
success with Jordan, and mounting difficulties with
the Palestinians characterized Rabin's government's effort to change
Israel's status in the Middle East. Despite some achievements
gain on both the Israeli and Palestinian side as a result
of the Oslo process or perhaps because of them, opposition
to the peace process intensified. The peace effort became
increasingly unpopular during three years, nearly 300 Israelis
were killed in terror attacks. Many Israelis began to have reservations
about the whole Oslo process and associates it a genuine
loss of personal safety. Gradually, the Israeli right wing's
opposition became increasingly aggressive. It included disturbances of peace, violent demonstrations,
call for civic uprising and unprecedented vehement
expressions against Rabin. Yitzhak Rabin is not my prime minister. His government is illegal and
illegitimate, and I will not comply with his orders,
declared Eliakim Ha'Etsni, one of the intellectuals
spokespersons of the settlers. The escalation of incitement
were expressed in demonstrations during which signs and banners were
raised proclaiming "Death to Rabin". Rabin the traitor, and in fire and
blood, we will deport Rabin". Posters depicted him as a Nazi SS officer. Leadership of the right wing extremist
camp which included public figures, rabbis, and politician, approved
every action aimed at interrupting the peace process and
fueling the flame of opposition. They did not protest when Rabin's
life was declared fair game, and when Jewish religious rulings were issued,
that permitted his persecution. They ignore and even revelled in,
the wonderful use of settlers that spread anti Rabin incitement
while disseminating poisonous messages against a democratically
elected prime minister. It subsequently became apparent that
out of this political atmosphere of delegitimazation and dehumanization
grew an extremist who believed that the only way of stopping the peace
process was to murder the Prime Minister. On November 4,
1995 a religious Jewish student, and an extremist right wing activist
shot and killed Yitzhak Rabin. Three bullets put an end to the life
of the Israeli Prime Minister at the conclusion of
an inspiring mass rally for peace that took place
that evening in Tel Aviv. The state of Israel went into shock and
grief. People mourned Rabin as a person,
but also asked themselves, what does it tell us about Israeli
democracy if such a political assassination could take place? 20 years after the assassination, and the Israeli society is still
processing this traumatic event. Despite numerous monuments and memorial sites for Rabin, disputes
over his legacy emerge every year, when different groups try to invest
meaning in his figure and activities. Should he be remembered as a war hero? The ultimate Israeli Sabre,
a patriotic figure, a martyr for the failed peace process or
a victim of political extremism. How to account for the fact that
shortly after his assassination, his political opponent of
the Likud Party won the election dominating Israeli politics for
most of the time since. Who can identify with Rabin's
message when peace is in ruins and the veteran Labor supporters as well as
the whole secular left wing camp is diminishing in size and influence. It seems that two unimaginable,
unrealistic scenarios need to actualize for an enduring
legacy of Rabin to be formulated. First is the secular, left-leaning
Labor oriented segment of Israeli society regains political power and
cultural prestige. And second is that the peace process
resumes and matures into an agreement. Through these two blueprints
seems as remote as ever, Israeli society will continue to contend
over the meaning of Rabin's legacy. It will continue to suffer from
a post traumatic stress disorder, regarding the assassination of a democratically elected Jewish Prime
Minister by a nationalist religious Jew, that occured in a presumably Jewish and
democratic state.
