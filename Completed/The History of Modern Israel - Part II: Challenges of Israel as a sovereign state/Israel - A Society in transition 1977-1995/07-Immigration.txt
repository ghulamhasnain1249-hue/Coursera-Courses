In principle, immigration of Jews to Israel,
was always on the agenda of the state. In fact, in the decades that followed the
mass immigration of the 50s and early 60s, immigration dwindled to insignificant
numbers, since most of the jewish of the world preferred to stay in
their countries rather than to move to Israel. The only place where some
Jews wanted to leave for Israel was the former Soviet Union. During the 70s,
some Jews organized Zionist group and demanded to immigrate to Israel. Most of their requests were rejected and
they suffered for their Zionist stance Some of them were arrested. Many lost their jobs and
positions in the society. Few managed to overcome the enormous
difficulties and come to Israel. These people were known in Israel and in the West as the Prisoners of Zion,
or Refuseniks. And their heroic struggle captured
Israeli media's attention, and that of the Jewish-American
establishment. However, most Soviet Jews were
detached from the Zionist group. In general, they assimilated into
the general society losing their Jewish religion and
their unique Jewish tradition and culture. Most of their offsprings
lost any Jewish identity. Some completely identified with
the communist regime, yet most of them, like most other Russian people were not
great supporters of the communist regime. At the end of the 1980s and during
the 90s, the Soviet Union collapsed. The breakdown of the whole system cause
enormous economic hardships to million on one hand and open the borders to
mass immigration on the other hand. Many people tried to leave the country for
the west, among them over a million Jews. A substantial portion of these Jews
either out of Zionist belief or by default since they had no other
place to go, came to Israel. Thus a new wave of Jewish mass immigration
took place during the 90s. More than a million people from the former
Soviet Union, with different degrees of affiliation to Judaism
arrived in Israel. The absorption of Soviet Jews in the 90s differed significantly from the absorption
of the mass immigration from Arab countries in the 50s. Some of the new immigrants learned about
Israel while they were still in Russia. Upon arrival,
they received the Basic Absorption Package from the state and
were sent to absorption centers. Veteran Russian immigrants
served as a facilitator for the absorption of the new ones. No attempt was made to turn them
immediately into native Israelis. These newcomers kept their language and
culture and formed Russian enclaves in cities that were comprised
of significant numbers of immigrants. The younger generation
integrated into Israeli society through the education system and
the army, but kept the cultural heritage and
the language of their parents. It is important to remember that in
contrast to the wave of immigration in the 50's, the population of the veteran
outnumbered the immigrants. And the state of Israel was
better equipped to carry out such absorption project. The economic growth of the 90s
enabled many immigrants to join the labor market,
especially those with professional skills. However, the older newcomers found
themselves working in temporary and marginal jobs. Russian journals, Russian cultural
institutions, Russian shops, and Russian social and political organizations
help the process of absorption by keeping a sense of autonomy and
particularism among the immigrants. Yet, the acculturation of the Russian
immigrants of the '90s into the Israeli society was complicated and
varied in pace. Youngsters assimilated
better than the elderly. Those with formal education better
than those who lacked education. Jews from urban European centers better than those from Asian
traditional background. Those who found way to get assistance from
various Israeli institutions benefited more than those who could not find a way
in the confusing bureaucratic system. Overall, this immigration
contributed to the Israeli society. Yet some of the immigrants didn't
perceive themselve as part of the Israeli society and
grew alienated from its culture. They never learned the language,
and in extreme cases, deteriorated to poverty, marginality and
even criminal activities. Many of them left to the west, and even
returned back to Russia after a few years. Another group of new immigrants who
arrived at the late 20th century were the Ethiopian Jews. The state decided to bring them
in a semi military operation, in order to save them,
from prosecution, hunger and suffering. The operation began with an exhausting
journey through the desert, which was imbued with Messianic vigor. From the desert,
they were airlifted to Israel. After the initial enthusiasm,
the problems of integration surfaced. The Ethiopian community differed in
significant ways from other Jewish communities. Its ways of observance was not accepted
by the state rabbinic establishment. Their dark skin signified their otherness. The language was unfamiliar, and racist attitudes impeded their full
integration to the Israeli society. Some of the relatives remained in
Ethiopia, and families were torn apart. The Ethiopian immigration was
not only a geographical move, but also a lapse in time from a tribal,
traditional, rural, pre-modern existence
to modern way of life. A small part of the Ethiopian
newcomers successfully integrated into the state of Israel, but many
remain on the fringe of the society. The religious-Halachic
problems endured over time and over generation thus
damaging the authority of the traditional leader of the community. Tension within the family generated
violence particularly against women. This violence is related
to the deterioration of the patriarchal authority of the father. High unemployment rate in the community,
lack of education, and frustration of living in
the periphery cause rage and anger. Yet, with all these difficulties,
the Ethiopians living in the country are here to stay and hope to bring more
of their relatives despite the fact that they are not recognized as Jews by the
religious establishment of the state and need to undergo religious Jewish
conversion in order to become citizens.
