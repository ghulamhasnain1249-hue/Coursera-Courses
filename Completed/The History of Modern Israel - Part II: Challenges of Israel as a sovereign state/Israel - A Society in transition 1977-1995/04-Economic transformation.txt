In the first decade,
the Israeli economy relied primarily on the public sector according
to state central strategy. This situation had already started
to change in the second half of the 60s when the private sector of the
economy increased its wealth and activity. The processes accelerated after the
political turnover, when the government moved toward a more liberal and
market-oriented economic policy. This move was consistent
with the ideological platform of the Likud Party and
correlated with the general economic trend of the Western
world that moved towards a more neo-liberal policy that minimized
government involvement in the economy. In Israel, the Likud government claim that liberalization will
contribute to economic growth, reduce bureaucratic inefficiency,
encourage competition, and fight favoritism, nepotism,
and public corruption. It assumed that private companies
operating according to the profit principle can run the economy
better than state or public owned company, which have no
incentive to be efficient and competitive. Thus, an extensive process of
privatization took place in Israel, since the late 70s until the present. The government diminished
its economic activities, cut or even dismantled many public and
state monopolies, sold public shares and capital to private businesses,
exempted investors, and entrepreneurs from certain taxes in order
to help them increase their profit, thereby, promoting economic growth in
a presumably free market environment. This liberal economic policy
was named privatization. Opponents of privatization argued that
such a policy spared the government from responsibility toward its citizens'
welfare, harm public solidarity and benefited the rich and the powerful at
the expense of the public at large. It would ultimately lead to growing
social inequality in Israel, which until the late 70s, was perceived as one of the most equal
societies in the Western world. Such a policy of privatization claimed the
opponents would substitute the pursuit of profit and philanthropy for
the pursuit of welfare and justice. After more than a generation of economic
liberalization and privatization, we can certainly discern the following
pattern of Israeli economy. Overall the Israeli economy has
experienced an unstable and an unbalanced economic growth, which had differential impact on
various segments of the society. Major economic sectors, such as
communication, finance, real estate, natural resources, insurance and even
certain parts of health, education and social welfare moved from public to
private ownership and management. The Israeli currency became fluctuated
and dependent on global financial market. From the mid 70's til the mid 80's,
Israel suffered a double digit inflation rate that turned into
a hyperinflation later on. It was overcome overnight. And since the 1990s and onwards, the
Israeli currency is stable and operates in accordance with the general monetary
tendencies of the capitalistic market. Israeli banks and other financial
institutions became mostly private, and the government cut its
share in their ownership. The big banks increased their profit,
and the system became a private duopoly that gave the owners and CO's and the CEOs enormous power over
the Israeli economy and beyond. Organized labor lost a significant
portion of its bargaining power. Many companies hired non unionized
workers, depriving them of social rights, and lacking a union that can take
care of there working condition. It increased the gap between
the unionized and non-unionized workers. The Histadrut, as the country's
umbrella union declined in its power and influence. It's still managed to maintain
some welfare shield to the strong segment of the Israeli labor forces,
but could not help those weak workers that are employed in private non
unionized companies and firms. Information and
service replaced agriculture and industry as a dominant,
central pillar of the Israeli economy. In the 80s,
most Kibbutzim faced bankruptcy, and as a result became dependent on private
banks and financial institutions. It severed their economic power and
harmed their image and prestige. Most of them adopted privatization, and that changed the very nature
of today's Kibbutzim. Today, they operate more like suburbs
than collective projects. Tax cuts and deregulation of business benefited wealthy
capitalists and owners of private firms. They could invest the capital
without government's control, thereby expanding their enterprises
in global markets. Israeli corporations, which most of
them were previously state owned, such as Israel Chemicals, El Al, Israeli
shipyard, Bezeq Communications, bank hapoalim, and corporations like Koor Industries
became private corporate giants. The owners became millionaires. They exploited their
monopolistic wealth and turned it into political power,
social influence, and cultural prestige. These tycoons were the real
beneficiaries of the new system. The social implications
of the economic privatization of Israel are clearly demonstrated in
increasing inequality and diminishing social solidarity. Today among Western states, Israel stands very high on
the scale of social inequality. The gap between rich and
poor is growing and is correlated with a gap
between literate and illiterate, center and
periphery, veterans and newcomers, Jews and Arabs,
Ashkenazi and Sephardi Nevertheless, most Israelis endorse
this new neoliberal economy, viewing privatization as an inevitable
process required for economic growth. Some even praise it as
a beneficial outcome that gave Israel a relative advantage
in the global economy. Others mourn the decline of
the Israeli welfare state and foresee a dire future,
if such a trend continues.
