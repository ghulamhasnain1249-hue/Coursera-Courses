The next thing I'd like to talk about is "the rule of law," Buddhism as "the rule of law"—the importance of law
and the commandments that are associated with it and therefore of course also of confession. And I suggest that the real key to Buddhism's popularity
lay in its complete and coherent system of karmic justice. We've already talked about karmic justice,
that is to say all actions are the actions of individuals. You don't inherit the misfortune or the good fortune of your parents. What you inherit and live out in this life is the result of your own past lives and your own past decisions and so what you do in this life will then
influence your fate in the next life. So it's that complete sense of karmic justice, that if justice isn't happening in this life
then it has to do with past lives and so what you do now can affect the just desserts that you will receive by the acquisition of merit in the next life. So what I'm suggesting:
it is this complete and coherent system of karmic justice which is central to the impact of Buddhism
on Chinese culture, society, and politics. It was a rules-based system in which, in this life or a next, all debts had to be paid but in which, also, the means of payment were clearly spelled out. Ordination, whether of lay persons or monastics, was called <i>shoujie</i> 受戒, "receiving the rules" from a "rules master <i>jieshi</i> 戒師," on a "rules platform <i>jietan</i> 戒壇." Confession and the concern for purity, as seen in texts recited at the start of every fast, before the confession began, also illustrate what the meaning of these rules is. And I quote from some of these texts
which are recited at the beginning of a ritual: "The observance of rules is pure like the full moon; body and mouth irradiate with immaculate light." Or, "washing my hands I get rid of impurities…
Fragrant water and smoke," incense smoke, "wash impurities… Perfectly pure, I receive the stick [of incense]. Perfectly pure, I give back the stick [of incense]." Here we can in fact imagine somebody being handed —somebody who's doing the ritual, central to the ritual— being handed the sticks of incense, offering them, and then handing them back
so that they can be planted in the incense burner. A final verse, recited at the end of the fast, after the litanies of confession, shows where this preparation leads. I quote: "A Buddha's appearing in the world
is the greatest joy—the greatest joy; listening to the Dharma and practicing it is happiness and joy." Now get the next line:
"Intermingling of crowds is the joy of <i>nirvana</i>." So in a way it's like describing the festivities
where these crowds come and they enjoy themselves together: "Intermingling of crowds is the joy of <i>nirvana</i>." "Beings do not suffer and have the joy of ease." Monks had a total of 250 rules to observe, nuns 500. This is just one little indication that Buddhism, even though it has a kind of egalitarianism or gender neutrality, at the same time is also quite patriarchal
and quite biased against women. We will perhaps go into that later when we talk about Song Buddism, but here you see one little indication of that. These rules were read out during the monastic fast by an elder monk, who paused "after each category of rules
[to ask] if anyone in the audience" —the monastic audience—"had transgressed them. If everyone kept silent,
he moved on to the recitation of the next category of rules. But if anyone acknowledged violating a rule,
he had to confess his fault publicly." Remember how Daoist confession was made
in the Heavenly Master community: A person had to go inside his oratory
think about his behavior that had led to his illness, what faults he had committed, then he had to confess these faults
so that they could be written down and sent off by petition. So a very very different kind of private
versus very public practice of confession.
