Lu Xiujing, the great Daoist reformer of the fifth century, dates I believe are 406 to 477, says that he "issued 1200 official edicts and 10,000 talismans to attack the temples and kill the ghosts, so that the people were cleansed, the universe was illuminated with justice, and the entire world was no more haunted by lascivious ghosts." "Lascivious" here is just that same word used for— just a word of opprobrium to negate the cults and the temples, forms of worship which were considered to be out of bounds, not according to the classic register of sacrifices. The <i>Lotus Sutra</i> supplies a simple prophylactic method for dealing with undesirable spirits. I quote: "As for all the ghosts and spirits of the 3000 worlds and those evil demons who wish to harm people, if one concentrates on pronouncing the name of Avalokitesvara" —that is to say Guanyin or Guangshiyin— "they will naturally be subdued and cannot make wanton offence." So that's the simple solution. Just repeat the name of Guanyin, focus and concentrate by repeating that name of that all-powerful bodhisattva and these much less powerful beings will disappear. Buddhist monks also used incantations and recitation of scripture, and both practices were adopted by Daoists. In addition to techniques both Buddhism and Daoism offered interpretations of ghostly phenomena designed to undermine traditional beliefs and fears. Lu Xiujing explains that ghosts in fact, they are just <i>guqi</i> 故氣, "old [energies] of the six heavens who claim to be officials, assume titles, and associate with hundreds of spirits
and the five harmful ghosts." They're not to be taken seriously. They exist but they're not to be taken seriously, because the religion of the three heavens has conquered this evil old religion of old ethers—of <i>guqi</i>—of the six heavens. So from these examples what we see is that elite religion by its exploration of the subject, its deeper understanding of the nature of the human subject, finds its way to respond to this ongoing anthropomorphic religion, expressions of fear and anxiety of the people. And we can imagine that these explanations found their way into Buddhist and Daoist sermons during the fasts attended by lay persons, and that these sermons in turn contributed to the spread of rationalized and interiorized
understandings of the world of spirits. At the same time, what the glimpses of popular religion provided by Poo and Lin reveal is that popular religion was thriving at all levels of society, and that it was already characterized by elements that would be central to its role in local society right down to the present, namely martial <i>ligui</i>—vengeful ghosts—who were housed in temples, were represented by statues and mediums, and demanded blood sacrifice in exchange for protection from demons.
