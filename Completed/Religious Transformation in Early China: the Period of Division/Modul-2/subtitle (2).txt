So just a moment ago, we were talking about, is it Confucian or is it Daoist? And we had to say both. And now we have to ask is it elite or is it popular? On the one hand, we see the conflict
between the religion of the elite —Luan Ba, Ge Xuan, clearly literate persons, officials, belonging to the elite, attacking
the religion of the elite [people]— and then they're incorporated into the popular religion by virtue of the fact that they become worshipped. We saw twenty out of seventy of the transcendents
in the <i>Liexian zhuan</i> are worshipped. Mao Ying is worshiped by the local people just like a local god. Ge Hong, from whom these stories come, has a whole series of—clearly elite point of view— attacks on adepts that he considers to be charlatans. For example, "those at the time who
recognized [what Gu was doing]" —one of his figures—"treated it as a joke and laughed, but when ordinary people 凡人 heard him, they all believed his words." So there's duping the people, that motif coming again. Li Kuan, another "Daoist," "proceeded to die inside his hut, but his followers said that he was a 'transcendent who had transformed his body and escaped by means of a simulated corpse.'" So just looked like a corpse, in fact, often many stories that then later on they open up the tomb and they discover that there is no corpse there anymore; there's just his staff or sword or something like that, and so it's a fake corpse and that "his death was not real." Ge Hong goes on: "Stupid and shallow people of recent times become attracted by a self-lauding person who says, "I possess secret books," and so cherish and serve him. Many ordinary people and even children openly claim to possess a Dao—possess a Way— "their reputations, thanks to their boasting and deceit, exceeding their actual attainment, while within they harbor greed, intent only on profit." So here again we see how this ties into the idea that these other kinds of real transcendents or real Daoists don't accept pay for what they do. It's not about profit, it's not about the economy of exchange, it's about the economy of merit. "These sorts of <i>daoshi</i>"—one of the earliest times that we hear that very modern term of <i>daoshi</i>, Daoist, but we have to immediately add that the
earliest Buddhists were also called <i>daoshi</i>, so it's not—we see the word <i>daoshi</i> back at this period— that it automatically means a Daoist. So "these sorts of <i>daoshi</i> of false reputation… ashamed of their own ignorance and pretending that their knowledge is broad and sufficient, in the end are unwilling to seek instruction
from those better than they are… Yet they will not content themselves with remaining silent, but become spiteful toward those who
really possess a Dao and slander them, fearing that those others' reputations will overtake their own." So elite or popular? Well, it's clear that Ge Hong situates himself
very clearly as a member of the elite standing over against even <i>daoshi</i> of the wrong kind. So it's not just an attack on local gods
and local religion and spirit mediums, it's also attacking —well, we can say charlatans—producers of new religions, to use a contemporary term, who can trick people— stupid and shallow people, as he calls them.
