So before we leave the topic of these <i>fangshi</i>, these self-cultivators and their new religion, these "masters of esoteric skills," as Campany calls them, we're going to look at another figure called Wu Meng 吳猛, who later on becomes part of a very
important filial piety version of Daoism. So Wu Meng tales show us something about what pre-Buddhist—before Buddhism entered China— what paradise looked like and what the world of the dead looked like. When one Gan Qing dies before his allotted time, Wu Meng tells his family to hold off on burying him—
don't bury him yet. And indeed three days later, he does wake up. He vomits putrid blood and after three days he was back to normal: "He said that at first he saw a dozen or so men arrive." Now listen, we're talking about the underworld and his experience of near death and
coming back to life and what he saw, so this is how stories are going to recount what that other world looks like. So he "saw a dozen or so men arrive; they bound and shackled him and took him to prison"— <i>diyu</i> 地獄, earthly prison. We also run into <i>tianyu</i> 天獄, heavenly prison, okay? So that's what happens after death, you go to prison. "Along with a group of a dozen or so others. They were being called by turns to face [the magistrate]." So just like this-world bureaucracy,
the other world has its bureaucracy. "And his turn had not yet come when suddenly he saw Lord Wu"—that's Wu Meng— "facing north pleading for his release." Very interesting! Facing north, not facing south. Remember we saw the Lord of Men, he sits <i>nanmian erzuo</i>: he sits facing south. He's facing north. North is where the most <i>yin</i> energies come from, this is where the cold and death comes from— winter, midnight, so everything negative. So he's facing north and later on we'll see that in this period there is developing of a whole cult of Beidi 北帝,
the Emperor of the North, who is in charge of exorcisms, okay? And so here we see that it's in fact already—already in place. So Wu Meng is pleading with them, so it's an underworld court where Wu Meng is like a lawyer and he's got his lawyer's brief, probably a petition sent by a Daoist. And he's pleading for the release of this Gan Qing and he wins. "The king therefore released him from his shackles" —that's the king of the underworld— "and ordered him to return. [On the way back] they were welcomed" —so he goes back, Wu Meng accompanies him back. "On the way back, they were welcomed and received at whatever offices and bureaus they passed, and everyone extended invitations to Lord Wu, who acted as if he were of equal status to them all." So here we have Wu Meng looking very much like a Daoist priest who's taken his petition down into the underworld, who's done his job as a court lawyer, saved his client, and then walks back with him, and he's an official, just like in this world, people invite each other back and forth and that officialdom. On another occasion, Wu Meng takes his disciples —notice: he has disciples, so he himself
already while he's alive has a community— so he takes his disciples and a regional official
into a famous mountain: "They saw an old sire sitting beneath a cassia tree using a jade cup to receive sweet dew"—a kind of ambrosia. "He offered it to Meng, who passed it around to all the disciples. Then they proceeded to a place
where they saw ranks of towers and capacious buildings, jade rooms and gold chambers" —well, we're in heaven now, we're not in hell— "tinkling and sparkling; the radiance dazzled the eyes, and there were too many precious gems
and jade vessels to recognize or name." Again, "they saw several persons conversing
with Meng as if they were old [friends]. [And] they were served 'jade salve' all day long." What's this "jade salve" stuff? Well, most probably what it is,
it is the product of an alchemical transformation which is then drunk as though it were the
elixir of immortality, something like that. But in any case, you clearly see a Daoist or proto-Daoist paradise imagined in this story.
