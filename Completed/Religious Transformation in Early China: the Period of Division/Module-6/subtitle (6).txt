So now we move on to Buddhist sacred geography, a chapter written by James Robson
who is now professor at Harvard University. I quote from him: "Buddhists in China settled primarily on mountains with rich pre-Buddhist histories." Here we see an example that we are familiar
with also in European history as Christianity spread through the Roman Empire. What did it do? It very consciously occupied other sacred sites. If you go to the Cathedral of Chartres, you can go into its underground and see a labyrinth which refers to ancient forms of cult. Okay? So taking over sacred sites,
and that's exactly what Buddhism does in China, settling primarily on mountains. Wutaishan 五台山, for example, in Shanxi, was called "capital of the transcendents," <i>xiandu</i> 仙都, before it became a place where monks went "in hope of gaining a vision of Wenshu 文殊 [Manjusri]." Tiantaishan 天台山 in Zhejiang had "beautiful and exquisite purification halls 佳精舍 inhabited by those who have attained the Dao" before Tanyou 曇猷, who died around 396, arrived from Dunhuang and "proceeded to
drive away the tigers and snakes (read: local cults) from the mountain and converted the mountain spirit [to Buddhism]." Many stories of Buddhist monks converting local gods, including mountain gods, to Buddhism. The sixth-century Tiantai patriarch Huisi 慧思 was attracted to the Southern Peak of Nanyue furen 南嶽夫人, the Lady of the Southern Peak, in Hunan. He was attracted to the Southern Peak's "numinous herbs and other pharmacopeia…" Very much like a Daoist immortal. "He hoped to enter into the mountain to attain supernatural powers and ingest magical substances in order to extend his life." Again very much like a Daoist, but his motivation was different: "So that he could preserve sūtras and meet Maitreya" —the god of the future, Messiah— "upon his descent into the world to usher in the new period of the 'correct dharma'." We talked, you may remember, about the period of the end of the dharma or the fake dharma. Okay? So he's waiting for the Messiah who's going to come down into the world and usher in a new period. So he wants to extend his life and preserve sūtras by in fact engaging in Daoist activities on a Daoist or what was once a Daoist and imperial mountain. Thus, says Robson, "the intimate
relationship between Buddhism and mountains —especially 'sacred' mountains— is primarily a Chinese development." It's part of the story of the sinicization of Buddhism. It's not something that's brought directly from [Indian] Buddhism. And the fourth-century development of landscape painting —that we've also evoked— and poetry confirmed and deepened this link. I quote: "The joining of mountains, Buddhism, and poetry is manifested in
a number of works from this period, but is nowhere more clear than in Sun Chuo's 孫綽 'Rhapsody on roaming the Celestial Terrace mountain'." Sun Chuo's dates are 314 to 371: <i>You tiantaishan fu</i> 遊天台山賦. Rhapsody is a specific poetic genre, so wandering on, roaming on this Tiantaishan in Zhejiang. This rhapsody "captures [very] well the divine nature of Tiantaishan and chronicles Sun Chuo's mystical ascent of the mountain, during which he meets Buddhist and Daoist saints and revels in the rarified spiritual atmosphere of the philosophical teachings of those two religious traditions." So this is a quite ecumenical roaming of one of the sacred sites of what was both Buddhism and Daoism, but will give its name actually to one of the schools of Buddhism—the Tiantai School of Buddhism. But Buddhism did not just imitate and occupy; it also transposed concepts and practices from India, "where Buddhist sacred geography was primarily keyed to sites associated with the life story of the Buddha and the distribution of his relics." Numerous mountains in China came to
be called Vulture Peak, Lingjiushan 靈鷲山, after "the important site where the Buddha preached." Others were simply called Feilaifeng 飛來峰, Mountain that Flew Over. But most important to the work of bringing India to China was "the miraculous discovery of relics, stūpas, and images connected with previous buddhas of
the distant past or with the King Asoka," who reigned around 268 to 232 BC, a very famous Buddhist emperor or king, who then becomes the model for Liang Wudi. In one of Daoxuan's two <i>Gantong lu</i> 感通錄 —Daoxuan, we've already encountered him
and I remind you of his dates, 596 to 667— he wrote two texts, both of which contain in the title <i>Gantong lu</i>. <i>Lu</i> is simply record, <i>gan</i> is that same term that we've referred to already, eliciting, stimulating the emergence of; and <i>tong</i> once again communication. So <i>Record of Eliciting Communication</i>. In one of them a whole series of sites in China are given "deep cosmic histories" by claiming that they had "relics that were deposited in China back in the time of the former buddha Kāsyapa." In the other <i>Gantong lu</i>, Daoxuan lists no fewer than 19 sites "that purportedly possessed Asokan relics," relics from the time of Asoka. For each site he tells the story of the "'discovery' of the relics and [of] the stūpas." Linking them to the Asokan distribution of relics meant that they were "considered to be a part of the Buddha himself." So here we see the transposition of India in the form of relics, so central to Buddhism's expansion in China. This text, the <i>Gantong lu</i>, the second one, carries the story right down to the Sui emperor Wendi, who reigned from 581 to 604, that is to say, he is the emperor who reunites China in the year 589. Emperor Wendi inserted Buddha relics in stupas throughout his newly unified empire according to, I quote from Robson, "a standardized protocol that included preparatory rituals and an injunction stipulating that the enshrinement rites were to be carried out at each site in precisely the same way at precisely the same time —noon on the 15th day of the tenth month of [the year] 601, a date which corresponded with the Daoist festival known as the 'lower prime'," or <i>xiayuan</i> 下元. That is to say, the 15th day of the tenth month, now of course the date 601. So here we have throughout his newly unified empire a statement that makes this a Buddhist empire by placing, like Asoka, these relics of the Buddha throughout the empire in stūpas, throughout the empire according to the same ritual at the same time of day. Among the sites selected for the deposit of a relic were the five sacred peaks, <i>wuyue</i>. The same emperor also ordered
"the construction of a Buddhist monastery at the foot of each of the five sacred peaks and other famous mountains in each province." There is no evidence to prove what I'm going to say now, but it seems highly likely that Sui imperial policy undid that of the preceding dynasty, the Northern Zhou, which had as we saw made the
same five sacred peaks centers of imperial Daoism. After mentioning an edict from the
last year of the Sui dynasty—that is 617— that, quote, "decreed that nine Daoist palaces were to be transformed into Buddhist monasteries and monks were to be ordained," Robson cites another imperial decree but this one from 724 issued by the Daoist emperor Xuanzong. I quote: "From the beginning there was the Changdao abbey on Qingchengshan 青城山 in Shu prefecture" —Sichuan, west of Chengdu. "Its original location was within the Qingcheng mountains. I have heard that Buddhist monks from the Feifu monastery 飛赴寺" —Flying Over Monastery— "seized it by force and converted it into a Buddhist temple 奪以為寺… The abbey is now returned to the Daoists and the Buddhist temple will have to revert to its former location off the mountain 山外舊所 in order that the Daoists and Buddhists
each have their own distinct place." Robson also mentions several cases of local gods who resisted the Buddhist "conquest," notably Guangong 關公: Guangong who is going to go on to a long career in which he becomes ever more important till he becomes the god of war of the entire Qing empire and of course also the god of commerce in many places, like in Guangdong province. Okay, so Guangong, at this point is still a local god. And when the monk Shenxiu 神秀, whose dates are 606 to around 706: So when Shenxiu destroyed his shrine —where? On Songshan 嵩山,
that is to say the central peak, the central sacred peak— Guangong "struck back and forced Shenxiu to rebuild his shrine." So what we've seen in this very quick introduction to Buddhist sacred geography is how it is part of the Chinese story of Buddhism. A Buddhist, Chinese Buddhism, with its focus on mountains and colonizing mountains which were already sacred before. But we see also how this then becomes a place of contestation between Buddhism and Daoism but also between Buddhism and the native gods, the meat-eating gods of these sacred mountains.
