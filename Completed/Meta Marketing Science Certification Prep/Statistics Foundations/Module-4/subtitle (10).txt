One of the essential skills for
a data analyst is the ability to group similar items based on shared
characteristics, such as categorizing customers by purchasing behavior or
students by learning styles. In this video, we will explore how cluster
analysis enables us to identify and group similar data points,
enhancing our understanding and decision-making in various scenarios. Remember, cluster
analysis is a statistical technique used to group a set
of objects or data points so that those within the same group or
cluster are more similar to each other in some sense
than those in other groups. Many different analyses fall
under this model type, and they have very different approaches. Here are a few of
the more popular options. K-means clustering is one
of the most used analyses, you may also come across Hierarchical
clustering, Fuzzy C-Means, Mean shift clustering, and
Density-based spatial clustering. All of these analyses create
clusters in different ways but they all do the same thing. The basics of creating
a cluster model are simple. First, we plot our data points,
then assign data points to one group or
another based on a calculation specific to the cluster model you have
run in statistical software. This gives you a visualization
of your segments and the ability to gain insights and
find connections throughout your audience. Now let's talk about assumptions. Each clustering analysis works differently
and uses different assumptions. So let's talk about
the clustering analysis, you are most likely to
use K-Means cluster. K means clustering calculates
specific points within your data and minimizes the distance from
them to create groups. K-Means clustering is the default
method for clustering and is used as a default in
statistical software. There are assumptions used
with K-Means clustering. The first assumption is
the minimum sample size. This can vary slightly, but you should have 50 participants for
every group you expect. If you think there are three groups, that would be 50 times 3,
or 150 participants. The second assumption of K-Means
clustering is sphericity. All this means is that the groups
will appear roughly round, like a circle or
sphere when you plot your data points. For example, the groups are roughly
round in this visual because the plotted data points are clustered
around three main areas. K-Means clustering positions
points at the center of your data through complex calculations
aided by statistical software. It forms groups based on the distance
from these central points, effectively clustering data that naturally
aggregates into roughly circular shapes. For example, this data looks like
it is in two vertical clusters, but that may not be what
K-Means clustering sees as it attempts to put it into circular shapes. K-Means clustering will break it up into roughly round shapes
as it creates groups for you. This is why it is important to
have roughly round groups to meet the assumption of sphericity. Luckily, this happens naturally unless
your data is in a strange distribution. If the groups in your data
are a different shape, you will need to find another clustering
analysis that fits your data better. The third K-Means assumption is
the homogeneity of variance, like before, all your variables
should have around the same variance. The last K-Means assumption
is that each group should have approximately the same
likelihood of happening or in other words,
an equal prior probability. If you know that two groups have a lot of
members and a third group only has one or two members, K-Means clustering will try
to even out the groups by artificially transferring members from the bigger
groups to the smaller groups. Now, you should be able to describe
the steps to complete a cluster analysis, which you can apply using
any statistical sftware. Get ready for the next steps and
start leveraging and analyzing real-world situations
using this type of analysis.
