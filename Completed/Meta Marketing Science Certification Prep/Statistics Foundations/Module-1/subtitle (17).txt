Imagine you're trying to
predict tomorrow's weather. You have a forecast based
on historical data, but then you notice clouds
gathering in the sky, so you decide to adjust
your prediction. This revision based on new
evidence is an example of Bayesian statistics because you change your prediction in
response to new information. In this video, we will explore Bayesian statistics and how they apply to
different fields from medical diagnoses to
financial markets. Bayesian statistics is a branch of statistics that interprets probability as a measure of belief or certainty
rather than frequency. It uses Bayes' theorem to
update the probability of a hypothesis as more evidence or information
becomes available. This approach allows for
continuously updating predictions or hypotheses based on incorporating new data, so what is Bayes' theorem? Bayes' theorem is a formula
that effectively asks, assuming A is true, which is the condition, what is the probability
of B being true? The formula for Bayes' theorem
is read as the probability of A given B on the
left-hand side equals the probability of B given A times the probability
of A all divided by the probability of
B. Bayes' theorem is important because
it allows us to refine predictions based
on new information. For example, if a patient
tests positive for a disease, Bayes' theorem can combine the initial likelihood
of the disease with the test accuracy to give a more accurate assessment
of the patient's condition. In the field of statistics, there are two main approaches, frequent test and Bayesian. Frequent test attempt to be
as objective as possible. When they look at
a frequency table, they only use the numbers
to predict an outcome. Bayesian thinkers, however, are subjective and
make assumptions. When they look at
a frequency table, they make educated
guesses called assumptions to try and
frame the data in context. Let's look at an example. We rolled a six-sided
die 12 times. In a perfect world, we would have rolled each number twice but that is not how
actual randomness works. What if we wanted to know the probability
of rolling a six? First, let's create a table with a column for
each possibility, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. Then let's fill
in the second row with data from an experiment
rolling the dice. Let's enter one time
for rolling a 1, three times for rolling a 2, two times for rolling a 3, zero times for rolling a 4, two times for rolling a 5, and four times rolling a 6. This makes 12 total
rolls for our table. A frequentist would
look at this table and say that based purely on
the data in the table, the probability of
rolling a 6 is 4 out of 12 total rolls or 1
and 3 and conclude the die must be improperly balanced while a
Bayesian thinker would look at this
table and say that if a game company made
poorly balanced dice, it would probably go out of business, which
is an assumption. Despite what the numbers
in the table say, the probability of rolling
a 6 is still 1 and 6. If you rolled enough times, the numbers would
eventually even out. Bayesian thinkers would assume that the sample size was too small to be accurate because it was not the
outcome they expected. Both frequentist and
Bayesian approaches have their benefits
and limitations, and data analysts often use both depending
on the situation. Frequentist methods
are preferred with full datasets as in predicting the weather
with historical patterns. Bayesian models are valuable
when data is lacking like updating a weather forecast with the latest
cloud observations. Like many other
concepts in statistics, choosing the best method
really depends on your data and which method best fits your
statistical design.
