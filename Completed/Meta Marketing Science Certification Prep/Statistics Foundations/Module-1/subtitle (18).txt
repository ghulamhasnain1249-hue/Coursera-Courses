discover how scatter plots and correlations describe
relationships between quantitative data. A scatter plot is a
visualization that compares two variables
which are numbers. One variable is on
the horizontal axis, and one is on the vertical axis. Each data point is plotted using the
location on the axis. Basic scatter plots
are great for visualizing the relationship
between two variables. They are commonly used
to explore connections between variables such
as age or location, and outcomes like sales
volume or expenditure. The relationship between
these two variables is called a correlation. A correlation measures
the degree to which two numerical variables move
in relation to each other. For instance, when
evaluating real estate, you might investigate whether a house's size relates
to its selling price. Does higher square
footage lead to a higher price or are there other factors
more influential? Understanding this
correlation helps in setting accurate price points and expectations for both
sellers and buyers. In a scatter plot analyzing
the housing market, each dot represents a home sale, plotting the square footage
against the sale price. Typically, we might
see a trend where larger homes tend to have
higher sales prices. While this doesn't
imply causation, which means that one
event causes another, it does suggest a relationship. As house size increases, so does sales price. There are three general types of correlation relationships that you will find as you begin to work with
scatter plots. The first type you'll learn about is a negative correlation. In a negative correlation, the dots move down from
left to right because as one variable increases,
the other decreases. If there's a
negative correlation between house prices
and square footage, it would mean that as the
price of the house increases, its price tends to decrease. This could imply
that larger homes in the dataset may be selling
for less than smaller ones. Possibly due to location, condition, market trends or other factors
affecting home values. The second type is a
positive correlation, and a positive correlation, the dots move up
from left to right because as one variable
increases, so does the other. If there's a
positive correlation between house prices
and square footage, we'd observe that as
houses become larger, their prices generally increase. This suggests a
direct relationship where larger homes
demand higher prices, perhaps due to greater
demand for space, more luxurious features, or
a more desirable location. Third, there is no correlation. When there is no correlation, there is no relationship
between the two variables, so there is no
observable line on the chart and the dots
are scattered randomly. Now, let's say you want to understand how related
these variables are. In this case, you will want to find the correlation
coefficient. The correlation coefficient
is a number between -1 and +1 that defines
how strong or weak the correlation is for
a set of data points. There are equations
and tests you can use to find the
correlation coefficient. Still, essentially,
the correlation coefficient looks
at the slope of the line and determines whether it's positive,
negative, or neutral. You have a strong
positive correlation if your correlation
coefficient is near 1. If your correlation
is closer to -1, you have a strong
negative correlation. If your correlation
coefficient is close to 0, this means the two
variables are not related. That said, you will rarely get
perfect values of 1 or -1. Most will fall
somewhere in between. There are many
different scales you can use to rate correlation, but a safe guide is to consider any correlation
coefficient below -0.7 or above +0.7 as
having a high correlation. Any correlation
coefficient below -0.5 or above 0.5 has
a medium correlation. Any correlation
coefficient below -0.3 or above +0.3 has
a low correlation. Finally, any correlation
coefficient between -0.3 and +0.3 does
not correlate. Whenever you think
about correlation, there is one thing that you
should always remember, correlation is not causation. This means that just
because there's a strong correlation
between two variables, does not mean one is
influencing the other. In a similar scenario involving house prices
in square footage, a negative correlation
might indicate that larger homes don't always
demand higher prices, possibly influenced
by other factors such as location or
market conditions. For example, if proximity
to the city center makes smaller properties
more desirable and expensive due
to convenience, larger homes further away might sell for less
despite their size. This suggests that while
size impacts price, external variables like location can significantly influence
the relationship. This can even be taken to extremes called
spurious correlations, or two variables that have a strong correlation
coefficient, but are not related. Let's illustrate these extremes with an exaggerated scenario. This is the example of the
divorce rate in Maine compared to the per capita consumption of margarine in

in your analysis, you'll learn more about
