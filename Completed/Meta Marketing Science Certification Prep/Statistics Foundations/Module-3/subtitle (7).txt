Previously, we discussed using an analysis to rule out chance
and observe differences. Essentially, calculating
what we call in statistics a p-value to see if changes between groups could
explain these differences. This video will guide you on using the p-value to identify statistically significant
differences between groups not explained by
accidental differences. A p-value measures
the probability that the difference between the
two groups was due to chance. For instance, if
morning workouts lead to greater weight loss
than evening workouts, the p-value will show if
this difference is due to chance or if there's a quantifiable reason for
the weight loss disparity. Another interesting fact is that the p-value is
expressed as a decimal, but should be interpreted
as a percent. So a p-value of 0.03
is the same as 3%. A p-value of 0.03 means there's a 3% chance we might be wrong in saying there's a difference when there actually isn't one. The question is, how willing
are you to be wrong? The great news is that
you don't have to decide. There are already conventions to decide what risk of being
wrong is acceptable. In statistics,
significant means that the analysis shows
a real difference between the compared groups. In other words, statistically significant means the
likelihood of error is low enough that we can
confidently say there's a notable difference between
the groups compared. What is the just right
amount of risk being wrong? Something that we call
Alpha determines that. Alpha is the cutoff value
that p should be compared to to determine whether differences are
statistically significant. The significance level or
Alpha is usually set at 0.05 to judge if a
result is significant. Changing Alpha affects
your test strictness and should only be done if you
fully grasp the implications. Let's see how the p-value Alpha and significance work together. To see if a difference
is significant, compare the p-value calculated
in the analysis to Alpha. Remember that Alpha
is often set at 0.05. If the p-value is under 0.05, it's significant, and you
reject the null hypothesis, suggesting there is
a real difference. If the p-value is over 0.05, the result isn't significant, and the null hypothesis stands indicating the
difference could be random. A p-value less than
Alpha indicates that we can be confident there's
a significant difference. This comparison method ensures
reliable calculations. Remember, if you fail to
reject the null hypothesis, this means that no
significant differences in the groups were
being compared. If you reject the
null hypothesis, then there are
significant differences in the groups being compared. As an example, if the
analysis of the effects of a high fiber diet on blood sugar levels gives
the p-value a 0.03, this is less than
the Alpha of 0.05, which means you reject the
null hypothesis and there are significant differences in blood sugar control
due to the diet. The ideas of p-value
significance and Alpha all relate
to one another. As you move through
