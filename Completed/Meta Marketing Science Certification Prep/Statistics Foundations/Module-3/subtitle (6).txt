When you make a guess or a hypothesis, the next natural step is to determine
whether your guess is correct. In this video, we will explore
hypothesis testing in statistics to better understand how to determine
if your hypothesis is correct. A hypothesis test is a form of
statistical inference that uses data from a sample to draw
conclusions about a population. Hypothesis tests are used with questions
like, is there a difference between demographic groups and how likely
they are to purchase this product? Can educational interventions enhance
student learning outcomes more effectively than traditional methods? Does a new health care protocol
improve patient recovery rates better than existing practices? What do all of these
questions have in common? They all compare two things to
determine if there is a difference. When comparing two or
more things in statistics to determine if there is a difference,
you use a hypothesis test. Now, there are two or more different
possible hypotheses for every test. The first hypothesis every test
will have is the null hypothesis. The null hypothesis,
which is usually written as H sub zero, is the default hypothesis
that always says there is no difference between
the things being compared. The second hypothesis is the alternate
hypothesis written as H sub one, which always says there is a difference
between the things being compared. Null and alternate hypotheses
are two sides of the same coin. If you accept one of them is true,
you must reject the other one as false. For example, if an analysis
tells you there is a difference between two groups,
then you reject the null hypothesis and therefore accept the alternate
hypothesis as true. If there's no difference,
you fail to reject the null hypothesis and therefore the alternate
hypothesis is false. Let's explore a common hypothesis test,
the AB test, using the null and
alternate hypotheses in action. An AB hypothesis test is a test
where the hypothesis is made about the relationship
between two datasets, and those datasets are then compared
against each other to determine if there is a statistically
significant relationship or not. Let's use Calla & Ivy, a small florist,
to see how an AB hypothesis test works. Calla & Ivy send out a weekly
newsletter about their promotions with flower facts and other tidbits to
a fairly large subscriber list. They use a standard
subject line each week and already have a high open rate, but
wonder if they should switch their subject line to see if it results
in more newsletters being opened. Remember, the null hypothesis is that
there is no difference in the open rate for the weekly newsletter
between different subject lines. The alternate hypothesis is
a difference in the open rate for the weekly newsletter between
different subject lines. Since you think the new
subject line will do better, you're expecting the null
hypothesis to be false and therefore the alternate
hypothesis to be true. You run the study and find the new
subject line did slightly better, but you aren't sure if there is actually a
difference or if it's just random chance. You run the AB hypothesis test analysis
to determine if there is a true statistically significant difference
that isn't just happenstance. After you run the analysis, it turns
out that there is no statistically significant difference in the open
rate between the two subject lines. Unfortunately, we failed to
reject the null hypothesis. The analysis tells us that the difference
we saw in the open rate was just random chance and that the two subject lines would
probably even out with a larger sample. For this particular example, the new
subject line resulted in 0.5% more opens. However, this might be a fluke. Hypothesis tests look at not only
the difference between the two groups, but also the variance of the groups. 0.5% could have been significant if
the variance was small enough, and 20% may not have been significant
if the variance were huge. This means that it is not only
the difference between the two groups, but the difference when compared to
how much the variance groups have.
