When did a friend's
positive movie review last make you ignore its
lukewarm online ratings? This example of social bias
demonstrates how the opinions of those around us can influence our choices
and perceptions, affecting decisions from movie watching to broader beliefs. Recognizing your
bias is important to completing statistical
studies as a data analyst. In this video, we will explore
the common forms of bias to recognize it and eliminate potential skewing of
the data from bias. Let's begin with
statistical bias. Statistical bias occurs
when a model, experiment, or survey is conducted
in a way that makes the result unrepresentative
of the population. One example of bias
is if a survey about city park usage only includes responses from
people living near the parks. What about the responses from residents of the city that
don't live near the park? The results of this survey may not accurately
reflect the views of all city residents,
demonstrating statistical bias. To further help you
be aware of bias, let's look at other common
types and how to avoid them. Survey bias is a
common type of bias. Survey bias is when
the actions you take while giving a survey
will skew the results. The order of answers or questions can bias
survey responses. For example, respondents
might favor the first option listed or be influenced by initial questions
about your company, affecting their later choices. Modern survey tools combat this by randomly ordering
questions and answers. Leading questions that suggest a specific answer
like would you prefer the delightful Product A or the disgusting Product
B skew results and should be carefully avoided. Avoid leading questions
by ensuring they are neutral and do not
suggest a preferred response. Recall bias occurs when respondents misremember
past events, like overestimating how often
they exercise last month. To avoid recall bias, avoid open-ended questions about the past or make such questions multiple
choice and specific. Another type of bias
is cultural bias. Cultural bias is the tendency to interpret or judge
behaviors, values, or phenomenon based on the
standards of one's culture, potentially overlooking
or misrepresenting the perspectives
of other cultures. Cultural bias occurs when interpretations are based on
your own cultural standards, leading to misunderstandings
when applied globally. For instance, phrase
that's meaningful in one culture may not
translate well in another. To avoid cultural bias, consider using local
survey conductors familiar with the
culture to ensure translations capture the
intended meaning and use localization groups to adopt content for
different regions. Another type of bias
is confirmation bias. Confirmation bias occurs when
you actively look for data, survey responses, or other information that proves
your hypothesis is correct. For example, if you search for proof that your favorite
diet is the most effective, you're likely to find
supporting evidence true or not due to confirmation bias. To avoid confirmation bias, collect data objectively without trying to confirm
your initial beliefs. Stay open-minded and skeptical to ensure a balanced approach. Now, let's explore
observation bias. Observation bias is the
idea that people will act differently when they
know they're being watched, so you may not get
accurate answers. Simply said, people may behave differently if they know
they're being observed, potentially leading to
inaccurate responses. For example, in a
study on productivity, employees might work
harder if they're aware they're being monitored,
skewing the results. Obvious signs of observation, such as cameras or note takers, can influence
participants' behavior, leading them to provide socially desirable
answers instead of their true opinions. To minimize this, use subtle
observation methods for sensitive questions and opt for anonymous surveys to
encourage honest responses. The final type of bias
is selection bias. Selection bias occurs when the process used to
select participants or data analysis results in a sample that is not
representative of the population. For example, if a study on exercise habits only
includes gym members, it may overlook the exercise
routines of those working at home or outdoors,
demonstrating selection bias. The best way to avoid this
is to make sure you use the random sampling techniques we taught you earlier
in this course. Let's use a brief
example of a survey from our pet store,
Inu and Neko. Carlos wants to write a short
survey to see if Inu and Neko's customers prefer their dog food
brand to other brands. Carlos asked, Question 1, do you like the delicious Inu
and Neko brand of dog food? A, yes. B, no. Question 2, what is
your favorite brand? A, Inu and Neko. B, Brand B. C, Brand C. Question 3, please describe how
your dog reacted the first time it had Inu
and Neko brand dog food. This survey is
spectacularly biased. Words like delicious make question one a leading question. Additionally, both
the first question and the first answer to the second question
introduce order bias in that Inu and Neko
is listed first. Finally, the third question
introduces recall bias, where the survey
taker is asked to remember a time they
may not be able to. All in all, this survey
probably needs some help. You now understand general bias and some of the common
types to avoid. However, many biases exist. So if you suspect
bias in your data, pause, review your work, and consider a second opinion. Remember, biased results
are worse than no results, since they should not be
used for decision-making.
