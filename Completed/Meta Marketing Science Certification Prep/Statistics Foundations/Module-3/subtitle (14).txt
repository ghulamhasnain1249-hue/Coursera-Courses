Every day you're faced with countless
possibilities and decisions from work attendance to the weather to
learning new skills like juggling. Your brain filters and makes assumptions
to help you focus on what it deems important, such as assuming you
wouldn't be paid if you don't work or that the sky will be blue tomorrow. These assumptions guide your decisions,
but if they're incorrect,
your choices will be too. In this video we will examine what
assumptions are made when using statistical methods and
how they influence the outcome of a test. Lets start by defining what
a statistical assumption is. Statistical assumptions are a list
of facts that an analysis requires to be true in order
to give valid results. Statistical assumptions
are conditions assumed to be true so that analysis results can be accurate. These assumptions help avoid creating
a new formula for every data situation. Different analyses have
their own assumptions, which we'll cover later as we
examine specific analyses and the assumptions that need to be made for
each one. Some assumptions are more
common than others, lets explore some of
the most common assumptions. First, we might need to assume that
the data fits a normal distribution, the normality assumption means expecting
data to follow a bell curve distribution. For example,
assuming students test scores are normally distributed helps apply
certain statistical tests, making this assumption key for
analyzing data correctly. Second, we might need to assume
there is a linear relationship, a linear relationship is a steady
relationship between independent and dependent variables. For instance, assuming that
study time consistently affects test scores dependent variable allows for
accurate predictions. You can see this in the graph,
as study hours increase, the test scores also
increase at a steady rate. This assumption is vital for
correctly modeling and understanding relationships within data. Third, we might need to assume
homogeneity of variance, the homogeneity of variance
assumption means that different groups being compared should have
similar levels of variability. For instance, comparing test
scores from two teaching methods requires both groups to have similar
score spreads to compare them fairly. This assumption helps to make sure
comparisons are valid and reliable. Everyday, our assumptions
help simplify decisions from believing we wont be paid if we
skip work to expecting blue skies. However, questioning these assumptions
is key to making better choices, especially in statistics. Being aware and critical of our
assumptions enhances the quality of our decisions when using
statistical tests.
