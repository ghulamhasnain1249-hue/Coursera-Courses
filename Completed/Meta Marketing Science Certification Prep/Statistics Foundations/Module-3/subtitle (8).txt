Confidence intervals help us
understand an estimates accuracy. For example, saying I'll be
there in ten minutes, give or take five minutes means you might
arrive between five and 15 minutes. This range for arrival time is similar
to a confidence interval in statistics, which helps us estimate where a true
value lies within a specified range. In this video, we will define confidence
intervals and their use in statistics. A confidence interval is a range
in which the actual mean, sometimes called the true mean
of the population, will fall. For instance,
if researchers say the average height of a certain group is 68 inches,
with a 95% confidence interval of 66 to 70 inches,
they are 95% confident that the true average height falls
between these 266 and 70. This 95% isn't just an arbitrary number,
it's widely used because it strikes a balance between
being sure about our estimate and acknowledging that there is
a small chance we could be wrong. It suggests that if we repeated
the measurement 100 times, 95 times of the 100, the true mean
would fall within our calculated range. Confidence intervals are important
because they provide a range that estimates where the true value
of a measurement is likely to fall considering possible
errors in the data. This not only helps in making
more informed decisions, but also in assessing the reliability and
precision of these estimates. They allow researchers, businesses and
policy makers to measure the uncertainty in their findings and
make decisions considering it. But how do we determine these intervals? This brings us to the alpha level,
or the level of significance, which complements the confidence interval. The alpha level represents the proportion
of times the interval would not capture the true population parameter. So if we are 95% confident,
our alpha level is 5% or 0.05, indicating a 5% chance that the true
mean does not lie within our interval. Similarly, a 99% confidence interval
has an alpha level of 1% or 0.01, making it narrower and
more precise, but also requiring more stringent
conditions or larger sample sizes. Last, if you were only 90% confident,
then alpha would be 0.1. A confidence level of 90%
is less frequently used, which is why it comes last on our list. Most data analysts want
confidence higher than 90%. Confidence intervals are calculated
using statistical software, but the calculations have
several important components. First is the standard deviation,
which measures the spread or variability of the data set. Next is the sample size. Larger sample sizes tend to result
in narrower confidence intervals, indicating a more precise estimate because
they accurately reflect the population. The mean or average of the dataset is at
the center of the confidence interval. Last is the margin of error, which determines how wide the confidence
interval should be around the mean. This was a lot of information but you now
know the concept of confidence intervals. Later, it's time to leverage spreadsheets
to complete these calculations.
