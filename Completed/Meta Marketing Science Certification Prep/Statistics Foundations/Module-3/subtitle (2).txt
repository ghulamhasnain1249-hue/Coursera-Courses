Now that you know what a research
question is, where it comes from and what makes a good one,
let's make a guess or hypothesis as to what might be
the result of our research. In this video you will learn
how to write a good hypothesis. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation
made on the basis of limited evidence as a starting point for
further investigation. For instance, using the health research
question used earlier in this lesson, does incorporating 30 minutes
of cardiovascular exercise into my daily routine decrease my
blood pressure over six months? A simple hypothesis could be, adding daily cardiovascular exercise
will lower my blood pressure. This isn't formal research, but remember clarity is key in
crafting your hypothesis. But what about the elements
that make a good hypothesis? Lets explore those. A good hypothesis will
state what will change. You can also think of this as your
dependent variable, if that helps. Something as simple as stating that you
will see a change in blood pressure can meet the criteria of
stating what will change. You could also include how it will change. For example, saying that blood
pressure will increase or decrease describes how we expect the dependent
variable of blood pressure to change. Finally, you should include
what will cause the change, which you can also think of
as your independent variable. In our blood pressure scenario, it could
be that daily exercise is the independent variable that will cause an increase or
decrease in blood pressure. This states that exercise will
cause a change in blood pressure. Now let's evaluate our sample
hypothesis on blood pressure. Adding daily cardiovascular exercise
will lower my blood pressure. First, it states that blood pressure,
the dependent variable, will change. So it meets our first criteria
of stating what will change. Next, it states that blood
pressure will lower, which explains how blood
pressure will change. So it meets our second criteria of stating
how the dependent variable will change. Last, it states that daily cardiovascular
exercise will be what changes it. This meets the final criteria of
stating what will cause the change. As you find yourself curious about
things in your everyday life, practice applying these skills by simply
asking the question in the same style as a research question and
then writing a good hypothesis. These tools aren't just for
formal studies, they're useful for exploring any aspects of daily
life you're curious about.
