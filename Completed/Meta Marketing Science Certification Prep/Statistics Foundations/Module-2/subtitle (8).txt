The real world is often messy because things do not fit
neatly into categories. The same thing can
be said about data. It doesn't always fit perfectly into the common
distributions we expect. A dataset may resemble
a normal distribution, but not exactly match it. In this video, we'll explore different data shapes
you might encounter, such as skewness, kurtosis,
and transformations. Let's start with a skew. Skew is when a distribution is leaning one way or the other. In other words, skewness refers to the asymmetry
and a distribution. A distribution without
skew is symmetrical. For example, in
normal distribution, both sides are symmetrical. However, if your data
has a positive skew, it means the bulk
of data falls to the left trailing
over to the right. It's called a positive skew
because you end up with a long tail on the
right or positive side. For example, a graph of puppy weights from a
puppy food provider, survey might show most puppies
are on the lighter side. Puppies are young and
generally weigh less, so most of the
weight will be low. However, some people
may not switch to other dog foods until their
pets are fully grown. You will still have
a few dogs with much higher weights that will be part of the
tail to the right. If your data has
a negative skew, it means the majority of
your data is on the right, with the long tail stretching in the negative direction
or to the left. Returning to that
previous example, this would be a dog food
formulated for older dogs. Most of them will be heavier
because they're adults. But you will still
have a few dogs starting on adult food
at a younger age or some that are just
naturally tiny won't weigh any more than
when they were puppies. Their lighter weights will
form the tail to the left. Just as skew is the data
being pulled left or right, kurtosis is when a distribution
is pulled up or down. Positive kurtosis would make the graph look tall and sharp, like survey results showing most pets get neutered
at a specific age range, with only a few
outside that range. Negative kurtosis flattens
the distribution, shown in a graph of dogs'
ages at a grooming service, with nearly equal
representation across all ages. There will be slightly
fewer, very young, or very old pets, as pets need to be groomed
throughout their lives. So they constantly
get dogs of all ages. The odds of the next
dog being groomed, being a specific age, are almost the same as the odds for any other age in
the distribution. These are guidelines. Some equations will calculate the exact amount of
skew and kurtosis, but you won't need to know
those for your everyday work. We have previously
mentioned that many statistical analyses
require a normal distribution, but there is some wiggle room. If there is a little bit
of skew or kurtosis, it can be considered normal
enough to run analyses on. The question is, what can you do when you have
an obvious skew? That brings us to
transformations. Transformations are ways
in which you can try to normalize your data
or remove a skew. To transform data, you apply a simple mathematical
equation to every value so that the data shifts and the numbers become a
bit more normalized. You'll learn more about
specific transformations and how to do them
in a future reading. That said, sometimes
transformations don't work. In addition, a transformation
cannot fix kurtosis at all. In these situations, you
can look for outliers. Outliers are data values more than three standard
deviations away from your mean, which may skew your data
to the right or left. Removing outliers has a chance
of normalizing your data. Remember to check
for these patterns before running analyses, now that you've seen
how different shapes can occur in the data. If your data shape is
too far from normal, or if transformations and
outlier removals don't work, consider using
statistical methods designed for non-normal data.
