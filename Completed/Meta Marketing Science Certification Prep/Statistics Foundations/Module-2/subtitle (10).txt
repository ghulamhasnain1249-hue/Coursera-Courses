Data driven decisions is
a term we hear more often as companies and leaders use it to define their
strategic choices. But what type of
data do they mean? In this video, we will
explore the variables that represent the quantitative data frequently used in
decision making. Quantitative variables
can be measured and assigned a meaningful
numerical value. Quantitative variables are measurable and meaningful
numbers like height, weight, or age, unlike numbers
assigned to categories, such as one for blue
and two for green. Quantitative variables
can be broken down into two categories, discrete
and continuous. First, let's talk about
discrete variables. A discrete variable has a certain number of particular
values and nothing else. In other words, a discrete
variable can only take specific values
like whole numbers with no values in between. Examples of discrete variables include the number of
books on a bookshelf. The number of books has to be a whole number since you can't have three
and a half books. The second type of
quantitative variable is a continuous variable. A continuous variable is
a variable that can take an uncountable set of values or an infinite
set of values. In simpler terms,
this means that a continuous variable can take
any value within a range, including fractions or decimals, allowing for an infinite
number of possible values. For example, the temperature in a room could be 22.5
degrees Celsius, 22.55 degrees or any
value in between. Now, here comes the tricky part. Certain variables
can be continuous or discrete depending
upon how you use them. A prime example is money. Counting money to pay
at a gas station is discrete because you
deal with exact amounts, including cents, but not
fractions of a cent. Gas prices at the pump are continuous because
they can include decimals like 3.579 per gallon, which includes nine
tenths of a cent. By understanding whether
data should be treated as countable units or measurable quantities with
infinite possibilities, we can better analyze and interpret the information
at our disposal. Remember, in statistics,
the key is knowing the nature of numbers and how they relate to
the real world.
