run a good experiment. Now, let's look at the
nature of the results that come out of an experiment and
how you can interpret them. Remember that we run experiments to try to prove a hypothesis. In advertising, this hypothesis usually is an expected
effect of our advertising. For instance, the Carousel
ad campaign I run on Facebook will significantly lift the number of purchases
of my product. The results of your
experiments will help you to accept or reject
that hypothesis. In fact, your
experiment will let you conclude without a doubt
that your treatment, the advertising campaign, is responsible for the lift
in sales you're seeing. Results from an experiment
consist of two parts. First, you will get a number of incremental results
caused by your treatment. In our example, the number of extra sales as a result
of our advertising. You get this number by
looking at the conversions in both the control and
the experimental group. The difference in
the conversions you see in the experimental and the control group is your lift and is a result
of your treatment. The second part of the
results is an evaluation of how powerful your experiment
and its result is. Depending on the size of
your test and control group, you will get a measure
of how confident you can be in the results
of your experiments. This measure is expressed as a confidence or
power percentage. It's a measure of the likelihood of causal results of your test. It represents the
likelihood that a test's outcome would be the same if the test was repeated again under the same condition. For instance, the results of
your experiment might show a 10 percent lift in results and a 90 percent
confidence interval. That means that
the likelihood of you seeing the same
result when you repeat the tests again under the same conditions
is 90 percent. A confidence interval
of 90 percent or higher is considered good to be confident in a
causal relationship between your ads and the
lift you are observing. Lift tests can help a marketer evaluate a campaign's
effectiveness and they can also be helpful in making strategic decisions about
how to use budgets. By running multiple experiments, you could evaluate two
conflicting strategies and decide on which one results in a higher increase in
sales, for instance. That concludes our
lesson on experiments used to test the
effectiveness of advertising. The type of experiments
we explored are referred to as random control
experiments or RCT, and they use a control group. In our next lesson, we'll discuss another form
of experiments, AB tests. It's a form of experiment that does not use a control group, but that can be super
helpful as a tool to optimize campaigns.
