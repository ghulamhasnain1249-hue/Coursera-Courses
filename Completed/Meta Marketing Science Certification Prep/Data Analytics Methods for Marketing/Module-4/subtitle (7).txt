In our last video, we talked about how there are two different types of attribution models that are commonly used, single-touch and multi-touch models. We already talked about single-touch attribution models, and while they're very common, we also saw that they're not a very realistic representation of reality. After all, in most cases, people will see multiple ads, often on different platforms, before they make the decision to buy something. Multi-touch attribution models try to better capture that reality, so let's look at them. In multi-touch attribution, we assume that all of the ads people see leading up to taking the desired action, like making a purchase for instance, have an effect on that action. Or in other words, all ads together are responsible for the result. That makes sense, but the question becomes, were all ads equally important, or were some ads more important than others in their influence on the results? Depending on how you answer that question, a different attribution model can be used. Let's look at the most common ones. First, there's the even credit model, in which equal credit is given to the touchpoints, impressions, and clicks that led to a conversion. Let's go back to Cathy. We talked about her mattress purchase in our previous videos. Remember that Cathy saw several ads before she decided to purchase her new purple mattress. In the even credit model, we assume that all the ads Cathy saw played an equally important role in her purchase decision. The time decay model gives credit to touchpoints, clicks, or impressions, based on how recently they happened. This model gives more credit to touchpoints that happened more recently, so closer to the conversion. In the case of Cathy and her mattress purchase, using this model would mean that the New York Times ad would get most credit for her conversion. Next would be the YouTube ad, then the display ad she saw on her phone, and the first ad she saw would get the least credit. Another approach is the positional model. In this model, first and last touchpoints, whether they're clicks or impressions, are given more credit, while the credit for the remaining touchpoints in the middle is divided equally. So for Cathy's purchase, both the New York Times ad and the Facebook ad would be given most credit, and the other ads she saw along the way would be considered less important, but they still are given some credit. All of the multi-touch attribution models we described so far are referred to as rule-based models. As the analyst, you decide on the rules up front, and then you apply the model to your data. So you can decide whether you will use a rule in which you give credit to the touchpoints evenly or not. When you use attribution analysis in platforms like Google Analytics, for instance, you get to select a model you want to apply to your data. But it's not always clear which model best applies to your situation. How would you decide between all these models? You really have to understand what influences your users most, and that isn't always easy. That's why you may want to rely on a statistical model rather than one of these rule-based models I just described. Statistical models take a different approach. In statistical or math models, you let the data speak, and you don't decide on a model ahead of time. Statistical models use all available data to determine which ads impact results. This data-driven attribution model then assigns fractional credit for a conversion to touchpoints based on their estimated incremental impact. So instead of using your intuition to choose a rule, a statistical model uses the available data to figure out which touchpoints have the most influence. Statistical models are more robust and often better at reflecting reality. Let's look at an example of how marketers might use attribution. Let's go back to Kala and Ivy. During the summer, Imra ran campaigns across Facebook and Instagram with the goal to get people to subscribe to her new weekly flower delivery service. The campaign used a variety of ads. On Instagram, she primarily focused on creating awareness for the service with video ads that explained how the service works, and different image ads showcasing the seasonal bouquets. Imra also used Facebook to show ads with a coupon. Imra is now preparing the advertising budgets for the coming year, and given the success of the flower delivery service, she wants to increase marketing spending. She's now evaluating what the focus of her campaign should be. She decides to use attribution. When using the most common last-touch attribution model, she finds that the ads on Facebook with the embedded coupon are driving all the conversions. So she could conclude that it would be best to put all her advertising dollars in a similar campaign next year. Imra understands that the ad on Facebook with a promo attached would not have been nearly as effective without the awareness generated by the other campaigns. So she decides to take another look at her attribution model. Instead of using a last-click model, she uses the even credit model. Applying that model shows that the awareness ads on the path to purchase were indeed important to generate conversions. So Imra decides to include another awareness campaign for the coming year. Changing the attribution model helped Imra understand the influence of her campaigns. But it would have been even better had she used a statistical or data-driven model. That way, she would not rely on her intuition of how the influence of different ads may be spread along her customers journey. You can rely on tools like Google Analytics, for instance, to do this type of analysis. But in many cases, you'll find that marketers will rely on data scientists or research agencies to help them out with this. Measuring advertising effectiveness across channels is quite complex, and getting expert help can at times be necessary.
