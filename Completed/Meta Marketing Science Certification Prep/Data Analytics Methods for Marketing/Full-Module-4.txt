Marketing Mix Modeling is the established measurement solution for holistic, cross-channel sales measurement. It can be used to measure the effect of a large set of variables and media on both offline and online sales.

In this lesson, you’ll learn when Marketing Mix Models (MMM) are typically used, who uses them and how they influence marketing decisions. You’ll also learn what the typical outputs of a MMM look like and how these outputs help in the decision making.

Who uses MMM?
CFOs, CMOs, GMs, Brand Managers, and Media Planners across different industries use MMM to understand the factors that influence sales and how to spend marketing budgets across marketing channels, brands and countries. 

There are specific circumstances in which the use of MMM is even more prominent:  

When a business has a majority of their sales offline, or majority of their spend is on traditional offline channels (TV, Print, Radio, OOH).

Offline sales influenced by marketing cannot be tracked in the same way we can track online. MMM can provide a view of what your online or offline media spend is delivering in both offline and online sales.

Businesses with a large marketing budget 

Marketing Mix Modeling is expensive and so to justify the return on a MMM project, marketing spend must be significant. 

Businesses with sophisticated media plans that are too complex to set up multiple individual experiments to measure each factor in isolation. 

MMM allows them to measure holistically within a single project. 

What questions can MMM answer?
MMM is a CFO/CMO’s tool to understand how to spend their money across marketing channels, brands and countries. It is a robust model that can help answer many different questions. Common questions addressed are:

How much in sales did each of my marketing investments drive?

What was the Return on Investment (ROI) of each marketing channel across all my brands?

How should I allocate budget by channel, by brand, by country going forward?

Where should my next marketing dollar go?

What media weight is optimal for each major marketing channel?

What’s the ideal mix of 15s v 30s ads, video v static ads, prime vs. non-prime TV?

Which creative strategy has produced the highest sales impact?

How would sales be impacted if I made X change to my marketing plan?

If I needed to cut my marketing budget by X%, where should the dollars come from?

Understanding the output from a Marketing Mix Model (MMM)
There are three components to the output you receive from a Marketing Mix Modeling exercise: 

1. Report of incremental sales from modeled variables

2. Return on Investment Analysis

3. Media Curves

1. Report of Incremental Sales from modeled variables
Marketing Mix Models identify and quantify the factors that have an impact on the KPI. 

The model tries to replicate the historical business metrics as closely as possible by using data on how different factors have driven business outcomes over time. 

The output shows the incremental value that each of the factors has driven over the modeling time period.

2. Spend and ROI
In order to get full insight into the efficiency of the marketing budget allocation, it's important to evaluate the incremental sales resulting from marketing efforts in light of the cost of those marketing efforts. 

In order to achieve that, MMM also reports the spend for the different influencing factors and the Return on Investment (ROI) for each factor.

The return on investment in a media channel typically diminishes as investment in that media channel increases.  That means that a marketer needs to keep in mind that their return on investment may start to go down as they invest more dollars in a particular channel.

In our last video, we talked about how there are two different types of attribution models that are commonly used, single-touch and multi-touch models. We already talked about single-touch attribution models, and while they're very common, we also saw that they're not a very realistic representation of reality. After all, in most cases, people will see multiple ads, often on different platforms, before they make the decision to buy something. Multi-touch attribution models try to better capture that reality, so let's look at them. In multi-touch attribution, we assume that all of the ads people see leading up to taking the desired action, like making a purchase for instance, have an effect on that action. Or in other words, all ads together are responsible for the result. That makes sense, but the question becomes, were all ads equally important, or were some ads more important than others in their influence on the results? Depending on how you answer that question, a different attribution model can be used. Let's look at the most common ones. First, there's the even credit model, in which equal credit is given to the touchpoints, impressions, and clicks that led to a conversion. Let's go back to Cathy. We talked about her mattress purchase in our previous videos. Remember that Cathy saw several ads before she decided to purchase her new purple mattress. In the even credit model, we assume that all the ads Cathy saw played an equally important role in her purchase decision. The time decay model gives credit to touchpoints, clicks, or impressions, based on how recently they happened. This model gives more credit to touchpoints that happened more recently, so closer to the conversion. In the case of Cathy and her mattress purchase, using this model would mean that the New York Times ad would get most credit for her conversion. Next would be the YouTube ad, then the display ad she saw on her phone, and the first ad she saw would get the least credit. Another approach is the positional model. In this model, first and last touchpoints, whether they're clicks or impressions, are given more credit, while the credit for the remaining touchpoints in the middle is divided equally. So for Cathy's purchase, both the New York Times ad and the Facebook ad would be given most credit, and the other ads she saw along the way would be considered less important, but they still are given some credit. All of the multi-touch attribution models we described so far are referred to as rule-based models. As the analyst, you decide on the rules up front, and then you apply the model to your data. So you can decide whether you will use a rule in which you give credit to the touchpoints evenly or not. When you use attribution analysis in platforms like Google Analytics, for instance, you get to select a model you want to apply to your data. But it's not always clear which model best applies to your situation. How would you decide between all these models? You really have to understand what influences your users most, and that isn't always easy. That's why you may want to rely on a statistical model rather than one of these rule-based models I just described. Statistical models take a different approach. In statistical or math models, you let the data speak, and you don't decide on a model ahead of time. Statistical models use all available data to determine which ads impact results. This data-driven attribution model then assigns fractional credit for a conversion to touchpoints based on their estimated incremental impact. So instead of using your intuition to choose a rule, a statistical model uses the available data to figure out which touchpoints have the most influence. Statistical models are more robust and often better at reflecting reality. Let's look at an example of how marketers might use attribution. Let's go back to Kala and Ivy. Changing the attribution model helped Imra understand the influence of her campaigns. But it would have been even better had she used a statistical or data-driven model. That way, she would not rely on her intuition of how the influence of different ads may be spread along her customers journey. You can rely on tools like Google Analytics, for instance, to do this type of analysis. No matter which term you describe them with, funnels are used to visualize a customer’s journey from their first interaction with your brand all the way to them actually making a purchase. Understanding what the steps in the funnel are will help us do our sales funnel analysis.

Dropoff in Sales Funnels
, a sales funnel could look like this: entry page, to the category page, product page, checkout page, and finally the thank you page. Stages of sales funnels
Regardless of what your funnel looks like, it will typically consist of about four stages that help us analyze and optimize funnels for maximum impact. 

Awareness: In this stage, we are trying to attract the right audience for our brand, so we want to ask questions like: what page are people entering on, where are they coming from and how long do they stay? Using this information can help us not only optimize these pages and push people to the next stage, but also to help evaluate whether people are getting the first impression of your brand that you’re wanting them to get.

Interest and consideration: In the interest stage, we’ve now attracted our audience and we’d like to keep their attention to prevent them from dropping out of the funnel. To this end, we can ask our data questions such as what are people searching for on my site? What categories or product pages are driving most traffic? Where are people dropping off without making a purchase or taking other action? This will help us keep their attention and can even help push us to the next stage. 

Conversion: At the conversion stage, you’re really looking diving into the purchase process. This is to understand a couple of things, particularly where do users drop off within this process and how might we retain them. This means examining potential pain points customers might be experiencing that are blocking them from making a purchase. For example, do they drop off when shipping costs are calculated? Or perhaps do they move backwards in the process at any point, which might mean that the checkout process is too complicated. Although these variables might seem like tiny details, they great food for thought as you consider moving your customers through the funnel and can even be easy fixes in reaching that goal. 

Retention: In the last stage, you’re looking at repeat customers, those that keep returning to make more purchases. Here in this stage the questions can look like:  how many of the visitors to our site are returning users and what pages do they visit? Asking what the repurchase rate is can also be very telling, because of course you want customers to come back and buy your products or services again!  

Tools such as Google Analytics can help you answer these questions, and the skills you learn in this course can help you make informed marketing plans after analyzing the data you collect. 

How do marketers use sales funnels? 
A marketer has two goals within the sales funnel, which are the following. 

Micro level
At the micro level, a marketer’s goal is to move an individual customer from one stage to the next (preferably all the way to purchase!). Focusing at this small-picture level helps us evaluate how people actually move through this process and what we can do to make it smoother for them. Marketers can step in at any point during the sales funnel through the website or other activities such as a phone call from a salesperson. 

Macro level
At the macro level, a marketer’s goal is to move as many people as possible through the funnel. This is where the insights from individual data points come in handy and give us a bigger picture of how we’re doing overall with our marketing efforts. 

Conclusion
Whether you use your sales funnel to get a better sense of how your customer base on aggregate flows through the process to help you make strategic decisions related to marketing action or to check in on an in-progress campaign, funnels can be a useful tool to fully optimize your marketing mix.

Quick overview 
As we’ve learned in previous atoms, sales funnels are useful visualizations of your customer’s journey from their first impression of your brand to actually making a purchase. Understanding what their experience is at various stages of the sales funnel can be immensely helpful in optimizing your marketing strategy, both as your campaign unfolds as well as afterward in assessing the success of your campaign. 

We’ve also explored what tools can be used in modeling your sales funnel, specifically Tableau. There are two ways to create a sales funnel using Tableau, which we’ll go over in this reading. Whether you need a very polished and aesthetically pleasing funnel visualization to show clients or your team or a simple one that you’re just modeling for yourself, you will have two new tools for your marketing analytics tool kit. 

Option 1: Fast and simple
Our first method of creating a sales funnel in Tableau is also the simplest and fastest. Let’s break it down step by step. 

1. Make sure you’ve uploaded your data to Tableau. 

2. Duplicate the KPI data so that you can mirror one for the funnel visualization effect. 


3. Drag both KPI measures to the columns tray. 


4. To get the reverse version, double-click on the x-axis of the left side of the visualization. In this window, you’ll then click on the checkbox called reversed under “scale,” then click out of that window.


5. Now you can add the “stages” of your funnel. 

6. Make sure that it shows correctly, convert it to a dimension by clicking on the triangle in the data pane then clicking on “convert to dimension.” 


7. Double-click stage and drag it over to the rows tray. 

8. Customize and polish as you wish from here, but you’re pretty much done! 


Option 2: Complex but visually appealing
This second option is more complex with more steps, but gives your sales funnel a more visually appealing, polished result. Let’s break this one down next.

1. Click on “new sheet” at the bottom of the page or simply start with a fresh dashboard. 

2. Drag your measure values to the columns tray twice to duplicate the data, just like with the previous method. 

3. Click on the right side copy of the measure values data and the little downward arrow. In that dropdown menu, click on “dual axis.” 


4. Remove the count of items in your file that Tableau may have grabbed, which are under “measure values.” 


5. Drag the “stage” data to the row tray. 

6. Instead of reversing the axis as in the first method, we’re now going to use a calculation to create our funnel shape: 

a. Click on the copy of your KPI data, then the downward arrow.



b. From the dropdown menu, click on “edit” and insert a subtraction sign before the brackets 


c. Click apply to create a set of mirrored numbers. It’s already looking like a funnel!

7. Customize as you wish. For example: 

a. Under the first measure values menu, drag in stage and the measure names. Set the stage as the color and measure names as the detail. Change this field from automatic to line so you have an outlined funnel in a spectrum of colors. 


b. Under the second measure values menu, drag in stage and measure names again. Again, set this to line, rather than automatic. This time, set the stage to color, but set the measure names to path, which will give you a nice, outlined funnel visualization.




Conclusion
Now that you have a grasp of using Tableau, you can start visualizing your sales funnels and planning your marketing strategies or begin optimizing currently in progress campaigns. As we’ve seen, funnels can be a quick and in-depth way to check in on not only your marketing efforts, but also your customer experience. Happy visualizing!
