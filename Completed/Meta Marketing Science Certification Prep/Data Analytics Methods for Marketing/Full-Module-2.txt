Profit margins
Profit margins are useful in many ways, from estimating a customer’s lifetime value to gauging the success of your business. 


There are three different ways of calculating profit margins. Let’s explore each one and how to calculate them.

Gross profit margin
Gross profit margin is an indicator of profits relative to production costs, basically looking at the total costs of goods sold, or COGS.  In gross profit margin, we use the total revenue generated by the goods minus the production costs for those goods. Gross profit margin is the easiest way to calculate profit margin, and often the most commonly used because it is so simple.

The formula for gross profit margin is shown below:


Let’s break it down even further with an example. Let’s say you have sold $1,000 worth of goods and you paid $400 to acquire those goods. First, you subtract 400 from 1000 to get 600, which is your profit. Then, you divide the profit by the total revenue, in this case 600 divided by 1000, to get 0.6. From there, our last step is to convert this to a percentage by multiplying by 100. This gives us a 60% gross profit margin. 


Operating profit margin
Operating profit margin is calculated as total revenue minus COGS minus business operating expenses divided by revenue times 100. Business operating expenses include, larger company operating expenses, and administrative expenses.


Let’s go back to our example where we’ve sold $1,000 in goods. We’ve established that we’ve  paid $400 to acquire those goods, but operating and administrative expenses cost us an additional $200 to equal $600 total. Then, we go through the same steps we did before. Subtract 600 from 1000 to get 400, divide 400 by 1000, to get 0.4 then multiply by 100 to get 40%, our operating profit margin.


As you can see, operating profit margin is lower than gross profit margin. It can give you a more realistic picture of your actual profit margin because it includes the costs of running your business as opposed to only factoring in the cost of the goods.

Net
Net profit margin includes total revenue minus COGS, business operating expenses, interest, taxes, and debt repayments, divided by revenue, times 100. 


Let’s go back one last time to our example where we’ve sold $1,000 in goods. Remember, we paid $400 to acquire those goods, and operating and administrative expenses added $200 to that, so we subtracted $600. Now, including interest, taxes, and debt repayments subtracts another $100 for a total of $700 out. Similarly to the other two profit margin formulas, we subtract $700 from $1,000 to get $300, then $300 divided by $1000 equals 0.3. Last but not least we need to make this a percentage, so we multiply by 100 to get a 30% net profit margin.


Our net profit margin is even lower than with the other two methods, but this is also even more accurate because we’re now including interest, taxes, and debt repayments into the mix.

Conclusion
As you can see, profit margin varies pretty widely depending on which formula you use. Each formula offers a different view of your business and is useful in its own way. 




























What is CLTV?
Customer lifetime value (CLTV) is the expected profitability of a consumer, and is forward looking, rather than an analysis of the current data to date. CLTV assumes that customers can or will change over time, whereas customer profitability only looks at how a customer has acted so far. CLTV allows us to focus not just on just the easiest acquisition, but also on acquiring long-term, profitable customers. Having a CLTV for a particular customer can even lead to increased return on investment by targeting customers who are likely to buy again and more often. 

What a CLTV includes
One of the most important pieces of forward-looking data that CLTV should incorporate is upcoming marketing programs, including any sort of current or upcoming marketing strategies as well as the expected results of those strategies.

Additionally, CLTV should look at the current and future marketplace, for example whether competitors have entered or left the space. Has the category grown or shrunk? Understanding the environment allows an analyst working on CLTV to anticipate any changes a customer might make. 

This brings us to our final consideration for CLTV, which is how have consumers changed? What are customers currently looking for from your product? Have purchasing habits changed at all for certain segments? For example, was our category hot and new but has now cooled down?

These considerations allow us to create a forward-thinking predictive model that is flexible enough to respond to both consumer and marketplace shifts.

Calculating CLTV
So far, we have discussed why CLTV matters. However, it’s also important to know that there are multiple formulas to calculate CLTV. The approaches that we discussed in previous atoms were relatively straightforward, but CLTV calculation can actually become a lot more complicated if you keep in mind some other factors that may influence the value of your customers over time. Let’s not get too ahead of ourselves though! We’ll start with the individual level, given that it’s the most granular and can be applied not just at the individual but at the segment and business levels. 

Individual CLTV
The most basic formula defines CLTV as the average value of a sale times the average number of transactions times the customer retention period times profit margin. Firstly, the average value of a sale should come from the individual’s transaction history.The average number of transactions in a customer lifetime, customer retention period and profit margin should come from data gathered across the business. Finally, to calculate the average value of a sale, pull transaction history for that user and divide by number of transactions. Let’s break this down a little further: 

If a consumer has spent $120 in 3 transactions, their average value of a sale is $40.

Using the gross profit margin formula (detailed in atom 3), we can say that the gross profit margin is 60%.

We can create individual CLTV as we’ve done using this example, or more commonly, we can create segments such as low, medium and high CLTV customers to harness the data to our advantage. This example uses the most basic formula, but other models bring in various other factors such as marketing and changes in the marketplace or consumer behavior.
