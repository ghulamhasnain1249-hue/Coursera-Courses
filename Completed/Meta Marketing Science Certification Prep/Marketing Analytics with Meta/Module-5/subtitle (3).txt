Once you have a clear idea of the business
goal and the KPIs you will use to measure whether the goal is achieved and
you've assessed the data. It's time to think about how you
will approach your analysis. Researchers often do this by
formulating a hypothesis in which they specify which variables they
believe will affect the outcomes. A hypothesis can then guide the analysis
and help explore and model the data. Formulating a hypothesis, especially important if you
plan to run an experiment. In that case the hypothesis will make
it crystal clear what your treatment variable is going to be in which outcomes
you will focus on in your measurement. A hypothesis is a statement or a proposed
explanation as a starting point for further investigation. It's an idea or an explanation that you
can test through study of your data or to experimentation. A strong hypothesis includes
the following elements. Who or the audience on the study. What or the behavior of that
audience that you are evaluating. Where, the location you're
evaluating this behavior in. When, in what time frame you're
looking at the behavior. And why, the rationale for
the anticipated audience behavior. Let's look at an example
of a strong hypothesis. For that, let's go back to Calla and
Ivy, the flower store in Amsterdam. Imra plans to run an advertising campaign
to increase the traffic to her website, her hypothesis is as follows. Ads targeted to women
between the ages of 35 and 64 who are interested in interior design
and live in cities in the Netherlands will increase visits to the website
during the month of August. In this hypothesis, the who is
women between the ages of 35 and 64 interested in interior design. The what is website visits. The where is cities in the Netherlands. And when is during the month of August. If you plan to run an experiment,
you will want to further fine tune your hypothesis and formulate what we
refer to as a test hypothesis. A test hypothesis is
a proposed explanation for an observation you can test,
usually in the form of if/then statement. A test hypothesis includes a null and
alternative hypothesis. The objective is to test if the null
hypothesis can be rejected and the alternative can be accepted. Let's go back to our hypothesis
Imra formulated for Calla and Ivy. A test hypothesis for
Imra could look like this. The null hypothesis would be that
advertising on Facebook does not increase website visits. And the alternative hypothesis would
be that advertising on Facebook does increase website visits. And Imra plans to set up an experiment
where she will test whether advertising on Facebook increases website visits to
try to reject the null hypothesis and accept her alternative hypothesis. A test hypothesis also
specifies an anticipated relationship between an independent and
independent variable. In other words, you specify the affect
an independent variable will have on an outcome, the dependent variable. In our example, the independent
variable is advertising on Facebook and the dependent variable is website visits. And the hypothesis specifies
a relationship between advertising on Facebook and website visits. In this case, Imra plans to use her
experiments to evaluate the effect of this one variable,
advertising on Facebook on website visits. It is possible to test the effect
of more than one variable, but it makes for a more complex test. Formulating your hypothesis
will make it clear for you what data you need and
what you will need to look for. This information will also help you
determine which measurement methods you should use. Let's look at how you can use your
hypothesis to select your measurement method in our next video.
