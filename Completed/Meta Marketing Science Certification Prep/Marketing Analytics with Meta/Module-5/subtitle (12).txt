Giving a presentation
can be daunting, even for the most
experienced presenter, and most professionals will be expected to do it at some
point in their career. From large company-wide
presentations to smaller presentations
just for your team, here are a few things to
remember to not only make your presentation impactful and to get your message across, but to also mitigate those nerves that will
inevitably arise. The most important thing
you can do is to prepare. Think through who your
audience is and what you would want them to take
away from your presentation. Think about what data you
want to present to them, how to tell your data story, and how to visualize
it in a unique way. Plan time in your schedule to
think through those steps. Write out your talking points and create your presentation in Google Slides or PowerPoint or other presentation software. Planning out what
you're going to say and how you're going to say
it is half the battle. When you are preparing
your presentation, it may be tempting to
put everything you would like to tell your
audience on your slides, but that's not a great idea. Essentially, you
want your slides to simply highlight what
you're speaking about, not to carry the weight of
the presentation itself. You'll be the storyteller
and your slides are there to support you and to stress the
main points of your story. Use only keywords or
short bullet points on your slides to do a simple visualization
of what you're saying. Otherwise, if you
have a ton of text, your audience will be
focused on reading your slides and we'll stop
paying attention to you. To help prevent you from putting too much text on your slides, keep your font size
to 28 or bigger. Anything that's smaller
can get hard to read on a slide and that way you will
stick to the main points. Focusing on highlights is also important for your visuals. Declutter them and use them to highlight
what you're saying, or else your audience will be distracted trying to
decipher your graphs. Also, don't read
from your slides. Use the presenter
notes feature to see your notes during
your presentation. Have summaries, or bullet
points, or note cards, or get to the point where you
know what you want to say well enough to let your slides
be a memory tool for you. When you start,
open with something that will catch your
audience's attention, a rhetorical question
related to your findings, or a story, or an
interesting fact. Grab the audience straight away and start them on the
journey with you. Don't spend too much
time on ramping up or introductions to the topic as your audience may drift off. Use simple story framework, which will not only keep
your audience engaged, but it'll help you move from point to point
with confidence. Start with a problem
you were trying to solve or what goal
you want to hit, then tell what actions you took. Explain what you found after
you took those actions and finish up telling how those findings will
impact future decisions. Make your conclusion as
impactful as your opening. In fact, circle back
to what you mentioned in your opening by either
answering the questions, finishing the story, or adding an interesting fact to the presentation unearthed. Always tell your audience you'll take questions
at the end, and then do so. Nothing derails a presentation faster than stopping to answer questions in the
middle that will probably be explained
by your story. What about nerves? Nerves are normal
and may actually help you propel through
your presentation. Just remember that
you'll be the most nervous just before
the presentation, but those nerves will
fade once you begin. Also remember that most people won't notice that
you're nervous at all. One way to mitigate nerves at the very beginning of
a presentation is to have an anchor or something that grounds you
so you can start. It might be pausing to take a deep breath or deliberately taking a sip
of water before you speak, or seeking out a friend
in the audience. Completing this simple task can build your confidence
before you even begin. Another way to mitigate
your nerves will be to practice your presentation
before you give it. You'll gain familiarity and confidence in what
you'll say the day off. You can also practice
intonations in various spots when
you want to hit your point home or see
if anything just doesn't work or it's too confusing
when spoken aloud. I've given many
presentations in my career, and very often I will practice my full presentation
out loud at home. I find that it helps
me a lot and while I always think that I will
easily roll through it, I often discover stumbling
blocks by practicing. I really think it's worth it to go through this extra step, especially if it's for a presentation I'm a
little bit nervous about. Practicing will also
give you a sense of how long your presentation
is going to take. You most likely won't have
an unlimited amount of time, and you don't want to find
yourself running out of time having to rush through
the end of your presentation. Time yourself and
edit accordingly. Practicing will also help you decide on which movements
to include as well, if you are giving your
presentation while standing. Don't grip the podium, but also don't wander and phase as you talk or talk
with your hands, which may distract
your audience. Some public speakers build
deliberate movements like a step forward into their presentation to
highlight their points. When you're giving
your presentation , don't talk quickly. You may do so because you're nervous and you want to
get through with it, but the audience will
notice and may not keep up. Slow down, allow for pauses to not only let the
information sink in, but to calm yourself as you go. Slowing down and being deliberate in what
you say can also help eliminate distracting
ums and ahs as well. Always be sure to maintain some kind of eye contact
with the audience, or make the audience
think you're maintaining some
kind of eye contact. Having a friend in the audience or someone who's attentive and nodding along is always
helpful to concentrate on. But pick a few people
in different spots of the room and look back
and forth between them, or look just over their heads. This keeps the audience engaged and along
for the journey. Even though you
practiced and have a general sense of how long
your presentation will go, always have some kind of clock
in sight so that you can adjust as needed and so that you can leave
some time for Q&A. Always be mindful of any tech issues that
may pop up as well, and be sure to answer the following questions
before your presentations. What's the room
setup where you're going to be giving
your presentation. Does it have a screen
for your presentation? How will you get your
presentation up on the screen? Will you have to bring your laptop and
connect to the screen, or does the room have an in-room computer you
need to use instead? If you'll be using
the in-room computer, how will you pull up
your presentation? Can you access the
folder it's in? Do you have to email
it to yourself? Can you access your email
through the computer? Do you need to bring your
presentation on a USB? Did you create your
presentation on a Mac, yet the in-room computer is a PC so the format
may be different? Will you have a remote
slide advancer, or will you have to stand by the keyboard to
advance your slides? If your presentation has a video and audio, will it work? How would you adjust the volume? Chat with someone who's familiar with the room
where you will be presenting and the setup so
you can prepare yourself. Walk through the
presentation situation in your head and use
this checklist. See where you might encounter any issues and try to
solve them beforehand. Day off always have a backup plan for loading
your presentation. You don't want to be without it. In fact, I always carry
a backup USB with the presentation just in case
I can't access it online. Once your presentation
is complete, offer to send out
a slide deck for anyone who wants to know more, and then plan to treat yourself to something nice
for your accomplishment.
