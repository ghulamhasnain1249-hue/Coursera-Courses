With the outbreak of
World War II in September 1939, the Zionist movement and
the Hebrew Yishuv faced a serious dilemma. Just a few months earlier,
Great Britain published the White Paper, which marked the transformation of British
policy towards an anti-Zionist stance. Since its publication on May 1939,
the Zionists were determined to struggle against Britain and
it's new pro-Arab policy. But it was clear from the outset of
the war that Britain was the major power fighting against the Nazis which were
the archenemy of the Jewish people. Immediately following the declaration
of the war, Chaim Weizmann, the head of the Jewish Agency, called for full
cooperation with Britain war effort and advocated to postpone any
anti-British activities. David Ben-Gurion the head of the political
division of the Jewish Agency and the leader of the Yishuv articulate this
policy in a famous public declaration. We must add the British in the struggle
as if there is no white paper and we must resist the White Paper
as if there is no war. This slogan meant that they assured
both support Britain war effort against Germany while waging a non-violent
struggle against its White Paper policy. The question was whether such a
contradictory policy could be implemented effectively. An examination of the Zionist British
relation during the war years revealed an attempt to
follow such a policy. However, it is clear that during
World War II, signs of cooperation with the British war effort outnumber those of
struggle against the White Papers policy. The cooperation was, above all,
an economic cooperation that enormously benefited the Jewish
community in Palestine. Mandatory Palestine became
the logistic center of British forces in the Middle East and
North Africa. Many Jewish factories and
firms produced food, uniform, and equipment for the British army. Jewish contractors build
military camps and bases, and perform maintenance tasks for
the army. Jewish workers were employed as
civilian by the British forces. Many services in need at time of war
such as hospitals, supply centers, and ammunition factories were provided
by member of the Jewish community. Such activities sparked
an economic boom in Palestine. It is ironic that in the most terrible years
of Jewish history more than half a million members of the Jewish community in
Palestine improved their living condition, and many of them even
prospered economically. The military cooperation was diverse and
broad. The largest militia organization
of the issue was the Haganah. Abiding to the appeal
of the Zionist leader, this organization seized all attempts
to confront British targets. It even started collaborating with
the British in training its member to become a local force able to halt any
possible German invasion of the country. The more militant organization,
the Irgun, which was under the control of the revisionist also followed
the call of Ze'ev Jabotinsky, the leader of the party and
seized any anti-British activity. It even rendered itself to
participate in British operation. The commander of the Irgun
was killed in Iraq during a British intelligence mission. The possibility of an invasion
of Palestine by the Axis forces seemed more ominous in 1941, 1942. During this period, North Syria and Lebanon were held by
the pro-Nazi Vichy regime. While in the East, a pro-German revolt
took place in Iraq where they even held the Farhud, a killing and
looting event against the Jews of Baghdad. The German army conquered North Africa
practicing the final solution method on the Jews in which Egypt,
100 kilometers from Alexandria, threatening to push on into
Palestine from the south. The political and military leadership of
the Yishuv devised an emergency plan whereby the Jews will
gather on Mt Carmel and confront the enemy from
there as the last fort. This imminent threat led to the formation
of the Palmach striking forces, as an elite combat force of the Haganah. The Palmach was trained with British help, that provided it with military knowledge
and supplied it military equipment. The Palmach units participated and commanded missions in secret
operation with the British. Following the Allied victory in
1942 the imminent threat against the Jewish community
in Palestine subsided. Consequently Zionist leaders increased the
effort to take part in the war in Europe. One of the heroic instances of military
operation were the 32 paratroopers sent to Europe and dropped behind the German lines on a dual
mission to collect intelligence for the Allies, and to support Jewish
resistance by helping to rescue Jews. Both missions failed. 12 of the paratroopers were captured, 7 of them including the poet
Hannah Senesh were executed. Despite that tragic outcome, this
heroic venture became a symbol of Jewish solidarity and bravery so
needed during the war. The high point of military cooperation
constituted of volunteering to the British army. The mandatory authorities openly
were creating offices in Palestine. But while the Jews joined the army,
the Arabs were reluctant to do so, and the leadership even
favored the Axis powers. The Zionist pressure to form a distinct
Jewish unit was only responded to in September of 1944 with
the establishment of the Jewish brigade. It included, 5,500 combatants who were
sent to Italy on October 1944 and who fought under the blue and
white Zionist flag. At the end of the war, the brigade
soldiers encountered survivors of the concentration camps, and they aided
Jews fleeing from Europe to Palestine. All in all, about 38,000 Jewish men and women from the Yishuv served in
the British army during the war. The overall military cooperation
of the British contributed significantly to the military skills and
capacity of many youngsters of the Yishuv, who belonged to the Haganah, the Jewish
semi-underground militia of the Zionists.
