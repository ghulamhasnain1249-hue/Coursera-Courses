At the end of the twenties,
despite intensive institutional and political development,
the overall demographic expectations of the Zionist movement
were far from being reached. The Yishuv survived,
but did not flourish. The Jewish population tripled
itself in numbers, but only reached 150,000 inhabitants. The aspiration to attain a Jewish
majority in Palestine seemed remote and far from reality. Yet the situation would change
in the following decade, duing to the fifth wave of immigration. It began around 1930,
intensified in 1933, and lasted continuously until 1939. Overall, more than 250,000 Jewish
immigrants arrived during those years. Thus by the end of the 30s the Jewish
community in Palestine amounted more than 400,000 people. This immigration stemmed from
increasing anti Semitism in US and Europe in general, and particularly from
the Nazis' rise to power in Germany. Since 1933, harassment and
discrimination against Jews in Germany reached a point where it
threatened their very existence. Following the enactment of
the racist Nuremburg law in 1935, their situation became dire and ominous,
causing many Jews to leave Germany. Only a few of them with
those Zionist agenda willingly decided to
immigrate to Palestine. Most of them, however,
came to Palestine as a different choice, since other places in
the world were closed to them. Overall, German Jews constituted less than
40% of the fifth wave of immigration. But in the collective
memory it is remembered and characterized as the German immigration. Due to the transfer agreement between
Zionist leader Haim Arlosoroff and the Nazi regime,
many Jews who left Germany were able to come to Palestine with
a significant amount of money. Consequently, an unprecedented volume
of private capital entered the land. The German immigrants came from cities and belonged to the professional highly
educated German middle class. Striving for integration into
German society and culture, they suffer personal crisis and personal tragedy when denied of the German
identity, citizenship, and culture. Many of them were lawyers,
journalists, artists, intellectuals, engineers, physicians,
authors, teachers, etc. Upon immigration they lost
their professional prestige. Not speaking the local language and
alienated from the oriental culture which they found inferior, many of them viewed
their move to Palestine as a tragedy. Some of them from exclusive German
enclaves in the new places could not or refused to learn Hebrew, and
sank into despair and melancholy. Others willy nilly adapted
to their new situation. However great the personal tragedies
were on the collective level, these new immigrants were a blessing for
the Jewish community in Palestine. In few years, such a professional
elite substantially enhanced the level of the issue of both,
technologically and culturally. Over-qualified lawyers, teachers,
professors, doctors, musicians, engineers and businessmen contributed
enormously to the whole society. The capital stimulated economic growth, the knowledge upgraded the level of
education and literacy, and the modern skill helped modernizing the Zionist
community in the city and the country. Indeed a distinctive Hebrew
culture emerged during the 30's. Mainly among the descendants of
the immigrants that were already born in Palestine, or came to the country
in their early childhood. This new generation of native Zionists was
born in Palestine, spoke Hebrew as its first and often only language, and felt at
home within their natural surroundings. They embraced a pragmatic and simple way of life,
emphasizing authenticity, rejected bourgeois manners, ridiculed and even
jeered at many form of phony politeness. This new type of Hebrew
native was called Sabra. They perceived themselves as
the embodiment of the new type of people, those who fulfill designers' ideas and
goals. They prefer to engage in concrete
tasks of national assignments, over intellectual contemplation or
ideological thinking. Taking for granted a national identity
of a revived Hebrew new nation, they view themselves as
the embodiment of a simple, young, modern society,
deeply rooted in the land.
