The third wave of Jewish immigration
began at the end of the first world war. Again, most of the immigrants
came from Russia and Poland. Many of them were already affiliated with
Zionist socialists organizations in Europe and prepared themselves to migrate. The turmoil and unknown future of
the Bolshevik Revolution in Russia, the pogroms against Jews during Russian
civil war and difficult economic situations caused 35,000 Jews to leave
their homes and to emigrate to Palestine. This immigration resembled the former one
in its social makeup of Eastern Europe, lower middle class and socialist ideology. Upon arrival in Palestine, the British
authorities employed the newcomers in labor groups that collectively
build roads in the country. Later on, many of them established
collective settlements known as Kibbutzim and semi-collective
settlements known as Moshavim. Yet the majority of them settle
in villages, towns, and cities. In 1924 about 70,000 immigrants
came from Eastern Europe, primarily from Poland, in what is known
as the fourth wave of immigration. Poverty and anti-Semitism, on one hand, prompted many Jews to look for
refuge away from eastern Europe. The law that limited
immigration to the U.S., on the other hand, forced many of them
to come to Palestine as a default. Half of the newcomers were urban families
that arrived with small amounts of money. They were peddlers, dealers, craftsmen and
traders that settled in this city. As a result of this wave of immigration, the urban sector of
the Yishuv increased rapidly. Tel Aviv and
Haifa doubled their population and the modern secular part of Jerusalem
grew significantly as well. New buildings project produce a short and unstable economic boom that ended with
disillusionment, crisis, and bankruptcy. Consequently, one-third of
the immigrants left back to Europe or found a way to enter North America. Numerous institutions, organizations, and voluntary associations emerged in
the Jewish issue during the 20s. Schools, youth movement,
trade unions, sport clubs, professional association,
self help NGOs, and public organization provide the social fabric of that actually
enriched Jewish society in Palestine. The Histadrut, a unique multi-dimensional
labor organization was founded in 1920. This umbrella organization gave
substantial political leverage to organize labor and translated socialist ideology
into an instrument of power and control. On one hand, it was a trade union
that represented organized labor. Working for collective bargaining
vis a vis the employers. On the other hand, it owned film and
companies, banks and factories, thus becoming the largest
employer in the country. It also provided the first medicare for
its member, and thereby giving them
basic health insurance. The Histadrut also functioned as
an educational and cultural organization. It founded schools and
designed the curriculum according to the working class ideology,
owned the most popular daily newspaper, founded the loudest youth movement,
sport clubs and owned a publishing house. The Histadrut resolved to
reinforce the organized and socialist labor camp of
the Jewish community. Yet it also served as a major political
institution of the Zionist Movement. It facilitated and carried out settlement
projects and supported immigration and absorption of immigrants by providing
them the shelter, temporary tenement and basic supplies. The Histadrut secretly founded an illegal
defence Jewish militia known as Haganah, which was under the control and
the authority of the Histadrut for the first decade of its existence. The revenue of that organization derived
from members tax, from the profit of economic firms and out of fundraising
from supporters of socialism. Almost any worker belonged
to the Histadrut. Moreover, many people outside the labor
camp joined organization because they needed the health insurance,wanted
to take part in leisure activity and took advantage of the educational and
cultural service that Histadrut provided. Hence, the labor organization
organized that daily lives of most members of the Zionist
community in Palestine. It practically functioned
as a state within a state. No surprise then that the founder and the
first general secretary of the Histadrut, David Ben-Guiron became the ultimate
leader of the Zionist movement shortly after he rose to prominence
in the 20s. Officially, the British authority granted some
degree of autonomy to both Jewish and Arab community in Palestine,
and the Jew translated it into an official framework of
democratic political institutions. All adult members of this community, male
and female, exercise the right to vote. Since the 1920s,
they could vote for an elected assembly of 314 representatives that
function under the British permission and the legislative body of
the Jewish community. This body nominated the National Committee
of 38 members which served as the executive
branch of the Yishuv. They represented the Jewish community
before they mandate officials, and dealt with internal political issues
such as education, religious service, sport, environment, and culture. They even levied
an internal voluntary tax. Many political parties
comprised the general assembly throughout the consecutive elections. Basically, they can be
divided into four camps. The labor block of the socialist party,
the civic block of liberal parties, the ethnic block representing Jews from
non-European society background, and the religious block of orthodox parties. The labor block was the loudest one,
but never held a majority position. It was able to control the national
committee through a coalition with one or more of the other blocks. In the 30s most socialist parties
integrated into one common party, named Mapai,
under the leadership of Ben-Gurion. While the liberal, ethnic and religious parties never
formed a comparable unified body. By employing the Histadrut's resources, the Labor block better mobilized people
for political and public activities. Therefore, despite the fact that it
never had the total majority within the general population, the labor block
became the hegemonic political and ideological body that led the Yishuv. During the 30s, alternative ideology emerged which
opposed the direction of the labor block. But they were never able to mobilize large
segments of the society to effectively challenge the labor's political,
ideological and cultural domination. Within the Zionist movement,
Chaim Weizmann, was able to provide the leadership, especially due to his connections
with British top leaders in London. Weizmann consolidated his
leadership by forming an alliance with the Labor politician of the Yishuv
though he himself was a liberal and never endorsed a socialist ideology. Weizmann's great achievement
was the establishment of the extended Jewish Agency in 1929 that became the official
political organization of the Jews. The Jewish Agency was equally divided
between the Jewish Federation, which were not Zionists, and the World
Zionist Organization headed by Weizmann. By including non-Zionists Jews body
under the umbrella of the Jewish Agency, the Zionist movement legitimized its
position within the Jewish world. By nominating the head
of the Zionist branch to be the chairperson
of the Jewish agency, the Zionist movement could carry out it's
agenda within a larger Jewish context. The Jewish agency would see to it
that both in London and Jerusalem and that's reflected the linkage between
the British administration and design this political establishment. In general, during the first
decade of the British mandate, the Jewish community in Palestine
slowly increased in size and gradually promoted a social and
political institution. 150,000 Jews settled on the land
mostly along the coastal line, Israel valley and eastern Galilee. The Yishuv developed it's social and
political infrastructure that would later turn into
a state in the process. Despite some tragic incident of
violent clashes with the Arabs that took place in 1920, 1921 and 1929,
the 20s in general proscribed peacefully. This apparent gradual development
of the Zionist Yishuv would be disrupted during the next decade.
