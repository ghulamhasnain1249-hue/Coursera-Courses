How did British colonialism, Zionist goals, and Palestinian resistance reshape historic Palestine before the UN involvement in 1947? ​Hi everyone, welcome back. In this lecture, we will discuss the situation in historic Palestine before Israel was established in 1948. ​We will reflect on how Palestinian society was heavily disrupted by changes in population demographics and land ownership under the influence of British colonialism and the Zionist movement. ​Let us go back in history to the beginning of the 20th century. ​Historic Palestine had a modest but healthy economic sector before all the conflicts and crises erupted in the area. ​There was big money in agriculture exports, construction, and transport. 
​Wealth was not distributed equally within Palestinian society. ​It included established and wealthy families of landowners and merchants. ​There was an urban-based middle class of professionals, and there were peasants and farmers in ruler villages. ​Roughly 70% of Palestinian Arabs were living in the countryside. ​There was also a thriving cultural sector in historic Palestine. ​In cities such as Jaffa and Haifa, there were newspapers and periodicals, there was literature, and there were cultural institutions and literary cafes. ​In Europe, at the end of the 19th century, the Zionist movement emerged. 
​It was a nationalist movement fueled by growing anti-Semitism in Europe. ​Since the Jews consider historic Palestine their holy land, they sought to enable the establishment of a homeland in Palestine for the Jewish people. ​During the First World War, the British government promised the Zionist movement in a public statement that they would be allowed to start settling in historic Palestine. ​The so-called Balfour Declaration of 1917. ​At the end of World War I, two European imperial powers, Britain and France, divided up the Middle East between them. ​As Britain assumed control of Palestine, they allowed the Zionist movement to establish a Jewish community and develop the economic and physical infrastructure for a future Israeli state in that area. ​Palestinian Arabs, meanwhile, pushed back against both British mandate rule and growing Zionist settlement, most notably during a revolt that lasted from 1936 to 1939. 
​The uprising was brutally suppressed by the British. ​Many Palestinians were killed and their homes were demolished. ​Zionist armed groups also participated in the suppression of the uprising. ​The Arab-Palestinians were hampered by divisions in leadership. ​Palestinian leadership was contested by political parties, powerful and established families, religious authorities, and other representative bodies. ​This is how the demographics in historic Palestine could change drastically from the 1920s to the 1940s. ​In the late 1920s, Palestine's population was roughly 93% Arab and 7% Jewish. 
​But during the British mandate of Palestine, almost half a million Jewish migrants arrived in Palestine, fleeing from Europe that was thoroughly antisemitic. ​At the same time, around 100,000 Palestinians were expelled from their homeland. ​By 1947, already one-third of the population in historic Palestine was Jewish. ​Not only did the Zionist movement facilitate Jewish migration to historic Palestine, but they also aspired to acquire more land during the British mandate period. ​Land purchases by the Jewish community often entailed the displacement and impoverishment of those Palestinian Arabs who had previously made a living working on the land of absentee Arab landowners. ​This had a negative impact on the Palestinian Arab community. ​After all, the growing numbers of Jews arriving in Palestine were meant to populate a state that was distinctively and predominantly Jewish. 
​And that state was to be established on top of what the Palestinians saw as their homeland. ​As tensions reached a critical point, Britain opted to relinquish its mandate. ​In 1947, the United Nations assumed the responsibility of finding a resolution to the situation in historic Palestine. ​Palestinian attempts to stop the Zionist colonization of their land were unsuccessful. ​This made it possible for the Zionist movement to successfully prepare the establishment of a Jewish state in historic Palestine by settlement and by force. ​In our next clip, we will further discuss settler colonialism as used by the Zionist movement.
