One reason why the Israeli-Palestinian conflict seems to be an never-ending story is that both protagonists are not only driven by mutually exclusive interests, but also by mutually exclusive narratives and traumas. In week 5, the last week of this course, we will first explore how these conflicting dynamics form the driving force behind the continuing violence and injustice in historic Palestine. In addition, in the second video of week 5, we will explore how and why it is possible that these dynamics are interpreted in divergent ways by different actors in different places and positions. Differently than non-Western countries, the support of Western countries for the Palestinian cause continues to be restrained. Officially, Western countries recognize the Palestinians’ right to self-determination and they support the two-state-solution. But, apart from financial support to the Palestinian Authority on the West Bank and limited diplomatic pressure on Israel, no further action is taken. Is this only due to Israel's importance as an economic partner and strategic ally? Or is more going on and are Europe and the US also guided by narratives that are sensitive to Jewish historical trauma, and coloured by religious and cultural factors?



How did trauma and collective memories influence the conflict in historic Palestine? The course thus far has shown that both Israelis and Palestinians claim the same land in historic Palestine. In this video, we will take a closer look at the historical narratives and experiences that drive the Israeli-Palestinian conflicting interests. Both Palestinian and Israeli interests are pushed by historical trauma. Jewish Israelis are driven by the trauma that was caused by centuries of persecution which culminated in the Holocaust during World War II. That persecution has left a lasting desire within part of the Jewish diaspora to establish a Jewish state. Historic Palestine is seen as the ancestral and religious home of Jewry where Jews can finally live safely without fear for death, indignation and exclusion. While the desire among Jews for a safe homeland is understandable, the settler-colonial way Israel was established and shaped in historic Palestine resulted in the trauma of the Palestinians. It meant the loss of what Palestinians see as their homeland. It meant their Mars expulsion and their daily humiliation under Israeli occupation and apartheid. These collective experiences make the Palestinians long for autonomy and safety in and access to historic Palestine. Let's go back to the Oslo Accords in the 1990s to illustrate some of these elements of Palestinian trauma and how they may clash with Israeli interests. Yasser Arafat's PLO was willing to compromise by claiming only 22% of historic Palestine including the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza. However, Israel was not willing to acknowledge any responsibility for the Nakba and therefore the Palestinian right of return. Many Israelis hold on to the idea that the Palestinians had voluntarily left their homes to go live elsewhere when Israel was established, although research by Benny Morris and other Israeli historians proved otherwise. In the 1980s a number of scholars began to look at new archival documentation which was being opened mainly in Israel but also in America and England and on the basis of this documentation we began to write a revised history of Israel or at least of the 1948 war and later I wrote a book on the refugee problem and others wrote about Israeli-Jordanian relations in 1948 and we became known as the new historians. Based on his research in the archives of the Israeli government, Morris showed that the Palestinians either fled out of fear of the impending war or because they were driven out by Jewish Zionists. In the negotiations in the year 2000 when discussing the right of return of Palestinian refugees, Israel was only willing to allow a token number of refugees to return so that the country could remain predominantly Jewish. If Arafat had agreed, he would no longer have been able to face his people. How should he have explained that he had agreed to erase the most dramatic event in Palestinian history? These examples indicate how collective trauma between Israelis and Palestinians create a zero-sum game where one party's gains are at the expense of the other. The Jewish Israelis cannot acknowledge the Palestinian experience without compromising on their ideals and the Palestinians cannot accept many of the Israeli claims without neglecting what they see as their national rights and historical justice. One solution would be to create a legal framework like international laws that transcend the direct interest of involved parties. The problem, however, is these laws can be disregarded or contested if the involved states have sufficient political support. For example, Israel to this day has neglected UN Security Council Resolution 242 which demands from Israel to retreat from the territories it captured in the 1967 war. Israel was able to ignore this resolution for so long with the support of its Western allies and by exploiting ambiguity in the original text of the resolution with regard to how much territory it should give back. At the same time, the resolution's text unambiguously emphasized the inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war. In other words, conquering land by war is never allowed. The everyday hostilities that result from this ongoing conflict affect the way ordinary Israelis and Palestinians see each other. For example, Israelis who believe that they have some divine right to the land often do not acknowledge Palestinians as a people but as an inferior Arab race. Many Palestinians, on the other hand, do not acknowledge the legitimacy of the State of Israel. They acknowledge its existence but only as a historical injustice and perceive its Jewish inhabitants as a threat. It is important to see that these negative beliefs are embedded in the traumas we discussed earlier. Thus, the historical Palestinian and Israeli traumas do not only result in conflicting political and historical views but eventually also in deep ethnic divisions in which the other is viewed as inferior, unreliable or vicious. Why is there so much controversy around this conflict in the global community? In this video we discuss how and why people around the globe have different understandings of the Palestinian-Israeli conflict. After Hamas' attack against Israel on the 7th of October 2023, American President Joe Biden issued a statement in which he promised that as long as the U.S. would stand, it would never let Israel be alone. Today the people of Israel are under attack, orchestrated by a terrorist organization, Hamas. In this moment of tragedy I want to say to them and to the world and to terrorists everywhere that the United States stands with Israel. We will not ever fail to have their back. We'll make sure that they have the help their citizens need and they can continue to defend themselves. A completely different tone was expressed in a South African statement stressing that Hamas' operation ensued from Israel's occupation of Palestinian land. South Africa even filed a case at the International Court of Justice alleging that Israel was engaging in genocidal acts. Such difference of opinion can be found all over the world at different levels, in political discourse, in the media and in conversations between ordinary citizens. How can we explain all this controversy? First of all, the Palestinian narrative is acknowledged mostly in non-Western countries which share a common history of Western colonial rule. They see their own experience reflected in the Israeli oppression of the Palestinians. This shared experience helps non-Western communities empathize with the plight of the Palestinians to see them as normal people like themselves who deserve to live in freedom and security. In addition, Islamic and Arab countries have ethnic and religious ties with the Palestinians who are, after all, also mostly Islamic and Arab. The religious connection is strengthened by the Islamic significance of historic Palestine which hosts the Al-Aqsa Mosque and other sacred Muslim places. But it is also the Palestinian suffering that push people to understand the Palestinian narrative. It makes them critically question the root causes of violence between the Palestinians and Israel. Armed Palestinian operations against Israel are in that context often seen as a response to Israeli oppression rather than terror that is by definition born out of anti-Semitic hate as Israel often claims. Increasingly, more non-Arab and non-Islamic people in the West seem to look at the conflict in these ways and therefore acknowledge Palestinian justice claims. This change has undoubtedly been enabled by the free flow of information on social media in the last few decades. However, solidarity and empathy with the Palestinians are mainly expressed on grassroots level or in critical media platforms. Such sentiments are less visible on an elite political level. Even Arab states keep friendly relations with Israel for economic, military or political reasons. European countries condemn Jewish-Israeli settlements in the West Bank but they do not dare to impose sanctions. Freezing the European Union's trade agreement with Israel would, however, cause great pain to Israel's economy. The Palestinian-led boycott, divestment and sanctions or BDS campaign which can count on increasing sympathy among European citizens is also not supported by the EU. But even if the EU were to sanction Israel such sanctions would have limited effect as long as the US remains firmly behind Israel. Where does this support come from? Why does the Jewish-Israeli narrative remain dominant in the West? Next to feelings of guilt about the Holocaust religious and cultural sentiments may also explain Western support for Israel. Israel was proclaimed by Jews of European descent. Many Europeans therefore feel a cultural affiliation with Jewish-Israelis. Evangelical Christians in Europe and the US are guided by the Bible which promises the return of the Jewish people to the Promised Land. And now that this prediction has come true nothing should undo God's word. Public discourse and personal perspectives in the West more generally see Israel as a country like us like a Western country that is also democratic and civilized. At the same time opposite views on the Arab and Islamic world can perpetuate Orientalism. The term Orientalism was introduced by the Palestinian scholar Edward Said. He noted that Europeans continued to view Arabs with a misplaced sense of superiority even after the colonial era. People in the West may still be guided by this caricature of the Arab in their views on the Palestinian-Israeli conflict. For them a solution to the conflict is near as soon as the Palestinians stop their violence but they often do not ask the question where that violence comes from as if violence is simply part of the Arab's nature.
