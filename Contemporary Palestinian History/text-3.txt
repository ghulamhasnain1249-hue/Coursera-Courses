The establishment of Israel resulted in a number of armed conflicts between Israel on the one hand, and its neighboring countries and the Palestinian people on the other. Week 2 will explain how these conflicts led to a stepwise expansion of Israeli territory. As a result, most Palestinians either came to live under Israeli occupation on non-contiguous pieces of land on a small portion (some 22%) of historic Palestine (the West Bank and Gaza), or as refugees and their descendants outside historic Palestine.

Specifically, this week’s first video will depict the armed conflicts that impacted the political geography of historic Palestine. The focus will be on the 1948 and 1967 conflicts. What happened in these conflicts and how did they impact the prospects of Palestinian statehood? The second video describes the plight of Palestinians that had to flee historic Palestine because of the violence and now form a worldwide diaspora of 7 million refugees and their descendants. 

Political and legal discussions about these issues will be highlighted where relevant. For instance, the first video highlights the significance of UN Security Council Resolution 242 for the appropriation of land by Israel in the 1967 war, and the second video mentions UN General Assembly Resolution 194 in regard to the Palestinian right of return.







​How did the wars of 1948 and 1967 shape Palestine from the Nakba to the quest for autonomy and justice? ​Welcome to the second week of the course. ​This session is about the 1948 and 1967 wars. ​We will discuss how these wars impacted Palestinian aspirations for autonomy and statehood in historic Palestine. ​On the 29th of November 1947, the United Nations proposed Resolution 181, ​which called for a partition of historic Palestine into two separate states, one Arab and one Jewish. ​Even though the Palestinian population was twice the size of the Jewish community, ​the resolution allocated 56% of Palestine to the Jewish state. ​Palestinians and the Arab countries rejected the partition plan but lost the UN vote 28% to 72%. 
​In November 1947, Zionist Jewish militias started ethnically cleansed Palestinian population from large areas in historic Palestine. ​This process was extremely violent, causing many deaths through massacres and otherwise. ​In May 1948, when the British mandate expired, the Jewish state declared itself as the independent state of Israel. ​After so much of struggle and effort and sacrifice and longing throughout through the centuries, ​has finally by the grace of God been reconstituted. ​This resulted in a war between a number of Arab countries and Israel. ​The resulting war had two defining aspects. ​First, Israel violently captured 78% of historic Palestine and it declared its state in that whole area. 
​The Israeli victory was achieved by the Zionist Jewish militias that were established and armed under the British colonial rule of Palestine. ​Second, these militias depopulated over 500 Palestinian towns between 1947 and 1949. ​More than 750,000 Palestinians were banished from their homes and they were replaced with foreign migrant settlers. ​To Israelis, the 1948 war meant independence. ​But for Palestinians, it had dire consequences. ​The Palestinians lost much of what they see as their land and they were displaced on a mass scale. ​This is why to Palestinians this event is called the Nakba, which means catastrophe in Arabic. 
​While 1948 is known as the Nakba, in 1967 matters went from bad to worse in a war that is known by the Palestinians as the Naksa or setback in Arabic. ​War in the Middle East. ​Israeli forces drive spearheads across the Sinai Peninsula, west to the Suez Canal, south to the entrance of the Gulf of Aqaba, ​breaking the blockade, capturing the west bank of the Jordan River and occupying the old city of Jerusalem. ​It was a war between Israel and the Arab countries of Egypt, Syria and Jordan. ​Israel defeated several Arab militaries and occupied the Egyptian Sinai and the Syrian Golan Heights. ​In addition, Israel occupied the rest of historic Palestine, covering the Gaza Strip, the West Bank and East Jerusalem. ​This worsened Palestinian living conditions and further diminished their prospects for recovering their land they saw taken from them two decades earlier. 
​Ordinary Palestinians, of course, also felt the consequences of the 1967 war. ​The war led to an additional displacement of hundreds of thousands of Palestinians. ​The Palestinians who remained in the West Bank and Gaza Strip came to live under brutal Israeli military occupation, ​which crushed political dissent and resistance, both armed and unarmed. ​Soon, Israel began to confiscate land to establish what would become a vast network of Jewish settlements across the newly occupied territory, ​which is a violation of the Fourth Geneva Conventions. ​To date, the areas that Israel captured in 1967 are called the occupied Palestinian territories by the international community. ​They are considered as occupied by Israel because under international law, countries are not allowed to acquire new territory by war. ​This is why UN Security Council Resolution 242 from November 1967 demands from Israel to withdraw from these areas. 
​For the defeated Arab states that lost the 1967 war, the domestic rebel effects were profound. ​In Egypt, for example, the 1967 defeat would be a fatal blow to President Jamal Abdel Nasser's vision of Pan-Arabism, ​the ideology of all Arab people being unified in a single nation-state. ​This, in turn, had an impact on the Palestinian National Movement. ​The Palestine Liberation Organization, or the PLO, is a Palestinian nationalist coalition founded in 1964. ​After the 1967 defeat, they deemed it necessary to change its focus from an Arab-led liberation strategy to a Palestinian-led strategy. ​Clearly, these developments have had lasting and profound effect on the Palestinian aspirations for autonomy and justice. ​The Palestinians lost around 78% of their land to the State of Israel. 
​In the rest of historic Palestine, they lived oppressed lives under Israeli occupation. ​Many Palestinians had to flee their homes and land and form the worldwide Palestinian diaspora of 7 million people living outside historic Palestine. ​In the next lesson, we will take a closer look at the Palestinian refugees in exile and the diaspora. ​We will talk about their plight and quest for return.

What does the right of return mean to the Palestinians? In this video, we take a closer look at the consequences for the Palestinian people who had to flee their homes because of the creation and expansion of Israel. After the Nakba of 1948, 750,000 Palestinians were expelled from their land. In the territory that Israel had claimed, around 90% of the Palestinians were forcibly displaced from their homes. Hundreds of villages were destroyed and depopulated. Whole neighborhoods in centers of Palestinian commercial, political, and cultural life, such as Jaffa, Haifa, and Jerusalem, were emptied. This worsened significantly in the Naxa of 1967 when Israel conquered the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip. An additional 300,000 Palestinians had to flee their homes. The Palestinians who were expelled or fled in fear were prevented from returning. Other Palestinians who happened to be outside of the country during these wars found themselves suddenly exiled. Right now, there are 7 million Palestinians dispersed worldwide due to their own displacement or the displacement of their parents and grandparents from historic Palestine. I am one of them. My family comes from the city of Al-Led. My grandmother told me in detail how in 1948 they were forced to leave Al-Led and walk to the city of Ramallah at the peak of a hot summer. My family, and all the others in the Palestinian diaspora, have a right of return to their land. Not only the people that were displaced themselves, but their descendants too. They have the right to return to any part of historic Palestine, including the area that is now called Israel. The Palestinian right of return has been affirmed by the United Nations in various resolutions. United Nations General Assembly Resolution 194, which was issued in 1948, states that Palestinian refugees should be permitted to return to their homes. And after the 1967 war, the United Nations Security Council issued Resolution 237, which called upon Israel to facilitate the return of Palestinians who were displaced due to the hostilities that year. However, Israel has never acknowledged the Palestinian right of return. As a result, millions of Palestinians remain stuck in refugee camps within historic Palestine and in the neighboring countries, particularly in Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon. The international community sought to help them with the UNRWA, which stands for the United Nations Relief and Work Agency for Palestinian Refugees in the Near East. Their mission ought to continue until a just settlement for the Palestinian refugee is reached. But their work is difficult, as the conditions and prospects of refugees in refugee camps are terrible. The displacements these tragedies caused are still felt and mourned in a genuine way by the global Palestinian community. This explains why the right of return is so important to them. But the right of return is likely to be an obstacle in solutions of the conflict that assume the continuation of Israel as a Jewish state. Because if Palestinians are to return to their land, it may result in that Israel is no longer a state with more Jews than non-Jews. Not all Palestinians in the diaspora would exercise their right of return if they could. Many have settled in other countries. For them, the importance of the right of return might be mainly symbolic. But there are also Palestinians who would like to return. The many Palestinians in refugee camps, for instance, are factually homeless, without any prospects on a better life. My grandmother, like many other grandparents, passed on to me the key of our family house in Al-Led before her death. In turn, I will pass them on to my kids, unless we will be able to open the doors together before my passing. In week four of this course, we will further explore possible solutions to the conflict. But for next week, we will discuss how the Palestinians tried to resist their plight and why the main diplomatic efforts to settle the conflict have largely failed.
