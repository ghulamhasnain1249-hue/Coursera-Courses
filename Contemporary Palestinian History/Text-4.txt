How have different forms of resistance shaped the struggle of the Palestinian people against Israel’s establishment and expansion? And in what way did the Oslo Accords hinder Palestinian self-determination and finally harmed the legitimacy of the Palestinian Liberation organization (PLO) as the most important representative of the Palestinian People? How have different forms of resistance shaped the struggle of the Palestinian people against Israel's expansion? In the first two weeks of the course, we discussed how Israel's establishment and expansion has had catastrophic consequences for the Palestinian people. Many Palestinians were killed in the 1948 and 1967 wars, as well as in battles on a smaller scale. Over a million Palestinians were displaced from their homes and land, and the Palestinians saw historic Palestine taken from them or occupied by Israel. In this video, we will discuss the ways the Palestinians have aimed to resist these consequences, which they see as grave historical injustices. Many people will associate Palestinian resistance with certain political organizations. One of these organizations is the Palestine Liberation Organization, or PLO, founded in 1964 with the goal to establish an Arab state in historic Palestine. The PLO has had different secular and leftist factions, including Yasser Arafat's party, Fatah, which led Palestinian resistance in Palestine and in exile. There are other well-known political organizations that have an Islamist ideology, such as Hamas and Islamic Jihad. These organizations were founded in the 1980s. Each of these organizations have formed different resistance fronts and adopted different resistance approaches. While organizations have played an important role in Palestinian resistance, that resistance has been manifested in divergent ways and on different levels. Let's discuss the main forms of resistance. A first category of resistance takes place in everyday life. For example, communal gatherings such as weddings or festivals can be a space for the singing of national songs, or songs that mock the Israeli military. Everyday resistance is also manifested in language use. For instance, ordinary Palestinians refuse to use the word Israel when they speak with each other. Instead, they refer to Israel with terms like the occupation, 1948, the entity, or the Zionists. Talking about the Israelis in this way is also a form of willful protest. A second form of resistance is civil disobedience. For instance, the Palestinians living in historic Palestine have sometimes collectively refused to pay taxes to Israel. Also, there have been Palestinian labor strikes across historic Palestine. And Palestinian prisoners inside Israeli jails have expressed their resistance through hunger strikes. A third category of resistance are popular uprisings. These include unarmed and spontaneous gatherings sparked by Israeli raids into Palestinian communities. Residents, typically youth, would respond by throwing stones and anything else to hand at Israeli soldiers and military vehicles. Uprisings can also be more organized and less violent. Through sustained campaigns of demonstrations against developments which threatens livelihood and property. For instance, villages have mobilized campaigns in the face of Israeli land confiscations and settlement expansions. There were two periods in which popular uprisings intensified into a sustained series of protests, riots, and violence. These periods are called the first and second intifada. The first intifada lasted from 1987 to 1993. The second one began in 2000 and ended in 2005. Intifada is derived from the Arabic verb nafada, which means to get rid of something or shake off something. In this case, the daily oppression by the Israeli occupation. A fourth category of resistance is armed and violent by nature. The best known form of this type of resistance is the use of bombings and suicide attacks in Israeli public places and transportation. This form of resistance has generated the most international attention and controversy. The use of suicide bombings emerged in the mid-1990s, but occurred far more frequently in the first few years of the second intifada in the early 2000s. In recent years, Hamas has taken over control of Gaza and was able to build up other forms of armed resistance against Israel. The Hamas-led military operation of the 7th of October 2023, called Operation Al-Aqsa Flood, also included a large armed raid from Gaza on Israeli targets. Meanwhile, in the West Bank, armed activities were also carried out by individuals who might have been affiliated with a Palestinian faction. Also, some Palestinian cities and refugee camps, such as in Jenin and Nablus, have seen the emergence of local, interfactional armed groups that resist Israeli forces and settlers. A fifth category of resistance is conducted by boycott, divestment, and sanctions. It is a global Palestinian-led movement for freedom, justice, and equality, through the use of international pressure and campaigning. The BDS movement urges non-violent pressure on Israel until it complies with international law by ending its occupation of Arab lands, treating Palestinian citizens of Israel equally, and respecting the right of Palestinian refugees to return to their homes. Since its launch in 2005, BDS has been able to challenge international support for Israeli apartheid and settler colonialism. It is with these five categories of resistance the Palestinians have resisted occupation of historic Palestine by Israel. Palestinians see what is known as Sumud as the driving force underlying the different categories of Palestinian resistance. Sumud means steadfastness and signifies the Palestinians' determination to remain or return to their land despite all the Israeli efforts to displace them. In what way did the Oslo Accords hinder Palestinian self-determination? Welcome to this session on Palestinian political leadership and the Oslo Accords. Historically, the Palestinian Liberation Organization, or the PLO, has been perceived as the sole legitimate representative body of the Palestinian people, of all Palestinians, everywhere. And until his death in 2004, its charismatic chairman Yasser Arafat, known as the father of the nation among Palestinians, was seen as the very personification of Palestinian resistance. The PLO has played a crucial role in Palestinian politics and liberation aspirations, engaging in diplomatic, political, and at times militant activities to address the Palestinian right to self-determination. In 1993, the PLO and Israel signed the Oslo Accords, in which they established a framework for the gradual transfer of power in the West Bank and Gaza Strip to a newly formed Palestinian authority. The Accords were seen as a historic breakthrough. But after PLO signed the Oslo Accords, its role and legitimacy among Palestinians changed drastically. What went wrong? How could an agreement that was to bring peace cause problems for the PLO? The Palestinian Authority was created as the institutional foundation for a two-state solution, a future Palestinian state next to Israel. Building statehood became the central objective in this project. However, the way statehood was pursued within the Oslo framework came at the expense of the Palestinian struggle. By keeping security, the Palestinian Authority would prove its capacity to govern the Palestinian territories and its capability to ensure the safety of Israel. The first of about 150 Palestinian policemen began crossing into the Israeli-occupied Gaza Strip. According to critics, the Palestinian authorities started arresting or intimidating individuals who were perceived as threats to the security coordination. There were allegations of political arrests, restrictions on freedom of expression, and use of force against activists or groups that were critical of the policies of the Palestinian Authority. The vast majority of the Palestinian people did not understand why the Palestinian Authority would police the West Bank while making no progress in achieving the Palestinian state that was promised by the Oslo process. In fact, while the Palestinian Authority was trying so hard to prove itself by keeping the Palestinian people under control, the Israelis continued colonizing the West Bank and East Jerusalem with new settlements and more settlers. The Israelis reinforced their presence there while pushing endless negotiations with the Palestinian Authority about marginal issues and offering it marginal forms of self-governance. All in all, the Oslo Accords have provided nothing more than a false framework of peace that only perpetuated the Israeli occupation and its abuses, but now with the active help and collaboration of the Palestinian political leadership. In that context, ordinary Palestinians and critical commentators have come to view the Palestinian Authority as a subcontractor to the Israeli occupation. The relations between the Palestinian Authority and Israel show more generally how complex it is for a supposedly national authority to build a state through collaboration with a settler colonial entity. If in such a collaboration the responsibilities of the colonial power are ill-defined and not properly enforced, it will be destined to disfavor the colonized people. The Oslo Accords were unable to solve problems with Palestinian political leadership. Personal and authoritative style of governance kept prevailing over democratic, inclusive, accountable, and more participatory approaches. Parliamentary and presidency elections only happened twice between 1993 and 2023, and there are huge trust and legitimacy gaps between the people and their political leaders. Power continues to be concentrated in the presidential position and affiliated security forces and establishment. Corruption is endemic and authoritarianism is structural. Despite these problems, the Palestinian Authority leadership remains fixated on the Oslo framework. This explains why the Palestinian Authority is often considered neither popular nor legitimate amongst the Palestinians. Therefore, if anything, the Palestinian political leadership and the Oslo Accords it maneuvers within has made Palestinians weaker and more fragmented. It has made the prospects of statehood, let alone equality, justice, and freedom, more distant. Addressing and reversing structural deficiencies of the Oslo Accords means adopting an accountability and people-first paradigm. This means that the Palestinians need to reinvent their political and governance system, to build or rebuild their democratic, representative, legitimate, inclusive, and effective leadership, and finally to redefine peace as an outcome of accountability, lasting justice, and equality, and reclaim its true meaning.
