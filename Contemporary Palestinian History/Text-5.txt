How do Israel's repressive policies in the West Bank and Gaza contribute to the claim that Israel is guilty of apartheid? In this video, we will illustrate what it means for the Palestinians to live under Israeli repression and how this might amount to the international crime of apartheid. The brutality of Israel's repressive policies has been acknowledged by established Israeli and Palestinian human rights NGOs, as well as organizations such as Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International. All these organizations have concluded that Israel is perpetrating apartheid. Apartheid is often associated with the former white minority regime in South Africa. But it's actually a more general notion that refers to severe and structural discriminatory oppression. It's also a crime against humanity. Let us return to the year 2000, when the Israeli occupation regime in the West Bank and Gaza entered a new phase with the outbreak of a second intifada. In the following years, until 2005, Israel killed more than 3,000 Palestinians. In the same period, suicide bombings by Hamas and other Palestinian militant groups killed around 1,000 Israelis. To prevent new attacks, Israel constructed a separation barrier on the border with and within the West Bank, consisting of high walls, wired fences, cameras, and armed watchtowers. The barrier also had a second purpose. At several places, the barrier penetrated deep into Palestinian West Bank territory, thereby laying the groundwork for the de facto annexation of 81 settlements that were illegally established by Israel in the West Bank. In 2005, Israel disengaged from Gaza. However, Israel blockaded the movement of goods and people in and out of Gaza, which caused social and economic hardship among the civilian population. This resulted in half of the 2 million Gazans not having enough food, despite receiving food assistance from the United Nations. In the West Bank, living circumstances also continued to get worse. The expansion of Jewish settlements, a dense network of connecting roads, and an ever increasing number of Israeli checkpoints, severely restricted Palestinian freedom of movement. Tax revenues collected by Israel on behalf of the Palestinian Authority were regularly stalled as a form of collective punishment. Across historic Palestine, dissident voices were silenced with military raids, mass imprisonment, and torture. In 2023, around 7,000 Palestinians were held in Israeli jails. More than 2,000 of them were held without charges or trial. And 200 of them were children. At the same time, settler violence against Palestinians, their villages, and their farmlands was increasingly allowed and even encouraged by the Israeli government. When the army intervened, it was only to protect militant settlers against Palestinians trying to defend themselves. Palestinians in Israel are better off. They, for example, have the right to vote and are allowed to establish political parties. But Israel considers itself a Jewish nation state. And as a result, Palestinian citizens of Israel continue to be second class citizens. Subjugated to institutional discrimination, including widespread restrictions on accessing land confiscated from them, home demolitions, and effective prohibitions on family reunification. The 1973 UN Apartheid Convention and the 1998 Rome Statute for the International Criminal Court mentioned that apartheid has three key elements. First, an intent to maintain domination by one racial group over another. Second, systematic oppression by one racial group over another. Third, the perpetration of inhumane acts such as sweeping movements restrictions, land confiscation, forcible transfer, denial of residency rights, and suspension of civil rights. All of these elements are clearly reflected in our earlier characterization of Israel's oppressive regime. Is it politically helpful to accuse Israel of a hideous crime like apartheid? Whether we make that accusation or not, it does not alter the fact that successive Israeli governments have been guilty of abuses that are not isolated, but part of a system designed to maintain the domination of one group over another group. Possible scenarios for ending this system and reaching a just settlement of the conflict will be discussed in the next video.









The 2023 Gaza War has made clear that there is more need than ever for a final and just peace settlement. But what could such a settlement look like? So far, the international political community has stuck to the so-called two-state solution that would create an independent Palestinian state next to Israel in the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Gaza Strip. This is the formula that both Israel and the PLO accepted within the framework of the Oslo Accords. However, there is an alternative to the two-state solution, which is the one-state solution. The one-state solution entails the transformation of the whole of historic Palestine into one democratic state with equal rights for both Israelis and Palestinians. In official diplomatic and political discourse, the two-state solution has been more mainstream and widely embraced. Two states along the 1967 lines where mutually agreed to swaps remain the best way to achieve equal measure of security, prosperity, freedom and democracy for the Palestinians as well as Israelis. The Palestinian people deserve a state of their own that's independent, sovereign, viable and contiguous. Two states for two people. Why is that the case? First of all, the two-state solution accommodates Israel's desire to remain a Jewish state. A one-state solution is usually dismissed by Israelis as it would likely cause Jews to become a minority. Another reason in favor of the two-state solution is that it corresponds to the legal status of Resolution 242 of the UN Security Council, which was issued after the 1967 war that we discussed in week two of the course. A third reason for preferring the two-state solution can be the troubled, traumatic history between the Israelis and Palestinians, which will certainly complicate the intense collaboration that is necessary within one state. However, proponents of the one-state formula claim that Israel's colonization of the West Bank and East Jerusalem has made the two-state solution much more difficult than the one-state solution. With more than 700,000 settlers in that area, it has become virtually impossible to form a contiguous Palestinian state on 22% of historic Palestine. A one-state solution would also do justice to the colossal Palestinian diaspora caused by the establishment of Israel in 1948. In the two-state scenario, the millions of Palestinian refugees cannot return to what they see as their land in what is now called Israel. Israel will not let them return so that the country can remain predominantly Jewish. However, under international law, and one can also argue morally and historically, the Palestinians have a right to return to their land, especially looking at the historical injustices suffered by the Palestinians due to the settler colonial ways in which Israel was created. In the eyes of one-state proponents, how legitimate can Israel be if its existence necessitates the continued suffering of the Palestinians? Although it's valuable to think about the pros and cons of the one and two-state solutions, both formulas remain highly hypothetical for the short term. In the decades following the collapse of the Oslo Accords, subsequent Israeli governments increasingly started to wonder why they should cooperate in favor of any peace option at all. Israel felt well protected from outside threats by heavily guarded borders with Gaza and the West Bank. By remaining vague about the future of the West Bank and not completely ruling out new peace talks, the Israeli government was able to maintain its occupation regime in the West Bank without too much international pressure. Meanwhile, subsequent Israeli governments were under the impression they had Hamas well under control in Gaza. However, Hamas' surprise attack in October 2023 gave Israel a rude awakening from a long-standing false sense of security. One might hope that this attack may help Israel finally realize that occupation and oppression do not offer a structural solution to the conflict. Increased international pressure will also be necessary to push Israeli policy in the right direction. A crucial role will be reserved for the United States, which, through its extensive financial and military assistance, can persuade Israel to change course. Neighboring Arab countries that are on good terms with both the Palestinians and Israel, such as Jordan, Egypt, Saudi Arabia and Qatar, can also play a significant role. The European Union, which is the main funder of the Palestinian Authority, is also able to influence Palestinian policy. On top of that, significant social and cultural change will be required to make peaceful and stable coexistence possible. For example, Israelis will need to acknowledge the suffering of the Palestinians as a displaced people, and Palestinians may need to learn to trust the Israelis after having suffered so long under their occupation.
